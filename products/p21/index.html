How to use this guide

You hiked before the baby came, and you want to keep going, together. This guide helps you plan short, happy family hikes with a baby or toddler, from birth to about age 4.

1

Read the short guide

Pages 3 to 19 cover trails, carriers, packing, clothing for heat and cold, food, diapers, naps, bugs, weather and safety. Each topic fits on one page.

2

Print the checklists

The packing list (page 8), the car kit (page 19) and the night-before list (page 23) work best printed and kept by the door.

3

Plan the hike

Fill in the family hike planner (page 20) the evening before: trail, timing around naps, turnaround time and who to tell.

4

Keep the memories

The hike log (page 21) and the milestones page (page 22) turn your first family hikes into a keepsake.

Your pediatrician comes first. This guide gives general outdoor advice, not medical advice. Ask your child's doctor about sun protection, insect repellents, feeding, how warm or cold is too much for your child, and anything else about their health. Follow your carrier maker's instructions for age, weight and fit.

Printing

Print at Actual size on US Letter. An A4 file and a low-ink printer-friendly file are included. Print the fill-in pages as often as you like for your own family.

The short guide

Why it is worth it, and what changes

Fresh air, new sounds, leaves to touch and a parent close by: a trail is a wonderful place for a little one. The hiking just looks different now.

Slower

Their pace, not yours

With a baby in a carrier you stop to feed, change and soothe. With a toddler on foot you stop for every stick, puddle and beetle. Plan for half your old pace, or less.

Shorter

Distance is not the goal

A 1 mile (1.6 km) loop that ends with a happy child is a great hike. Start short, then add a little each time it goes well.

More stops

Breaks are part of the plan

Expect a stop every 20 to 45 minutes. Choose trails with benches, flat rocks or a shady spot to sit, and treat stops as the fun part.

More gear

A heavier pack

Diapers, spare clothes, snacks and a carrier add weight. Pack carefully (pages 7 and 8) and let the second adult carry the bag when there is one.

A few family rules that help

  1. The hike is for the child, too. Stop where they want to stop.
  2. Leave while it is still fun. Turning back early is a success, not a failure.
  3. Keep it simple: the same easy trail in every season is a new hike each time.
  4. Tell someone where you are going and when you will be back, every time.

Many parents find that the first few outings feel like a lot of work. It gets easier quickly: you will learn your routine, your pack gets organized, and your child starts to recognize "hike days" and look forward to them.

Your first family hike: picking the trail

The best first trail is short, easy and close to home, with a way out if the day goes sideways.

Look for

  • 1 to 2 miles (1.5 to 3 km) in total
  • Little climbing, wide and even path
  • A loop or an easy out-and-back
  • Restrooms at the trailhead
  • Shade, benches or picnic tables
  • Something to see: a pond, a creek, a meadow
  • A short drive from home

Skip for now

  • Steep drop-offs and cliff edges
  • Fast or deep water crossings
  • Rocky scrambles and narrow ledges
  • No shade on a hot day
  • Long trails with no shortcut back
  • Busy mountain bike trails
  • Trails you have never hiked yourself

Where to find family-friendly trails

Local and county parks

Nature centers and park trails are often short, well marked and close to parking and restrooms.

State and provincial parks

Many list easy or accessible trails on their official websites, with maps and distances.

National parks

Park pages on nps.gov (US) and parks.canada.ca list short walks, nature trails and current closures.

Land trusts and nature preserves

Small local preserves often have quiet, easy trails that rarely get busy.

Check the official page for the trail on the day you go: closures, construction and seasonal hazards change.

Carriers by age

The right carrier changes as your child grows. Always follow the maker's age, weight and fit rules in the manual for your carrier.

Wrap or sling

Often the newborn months

Soft fabric that holds a small baby high on your chest. Light and cozy, but it takes practice to tie safely.

Best for very short, flat walks.

Soft structured carrier

Babies and toddlers

Buckles and a padded waist belt. Many work on the front for small babies and on the back once the maker allows it.

The most common choice for easy trails.

Frame carrier

Once baby sits well alone

A hiking pack with a seat for the child. Makers usually require steady head control and sitting without help; check yours.

Best for longer hikes with a toddler.

Fit check for wraps, slings and front carriers (tick each time)

T

Tight

Snug enough to hold baby close to you; nothing slack or sagging.

I

In view at all times

You can see your baby's face by simply looking down.

C

Close enough to kiss

Baby's head is high enough that you can kiss the top of it.

K

Keep chin off the chest

There is at least a finger's width under baby's chin, so the airway stays open.

S

Supported back

Baby's back is supported in a natural position, tummy and chest against you.

Trip and fall safety. Your balance changes with a child on your body. Watch every step, use trekking poles on uneven ground if they help, never bend at the waist with a frame carrier on (bend at the knees), and keep the frame carrier's harness fastened whenever the child is in it.

The short guide

When toddlers walk

Once your child is walking, the trail becomes theirs. They will not walk far or fast, and that is fine.

Short
walk on their own feet

Many young toddlers walk well under a mile (1.6 km) with lots of stops; older preschoolers often go further. Every child is different.

Carry
a plan B for tired legs

Bring a carrier on any hike longer than your child can walk. Tired legs can arrive suddenly, often on the way back.

Slow
is the real goal

Let them squat, look and touch. Curiosity is what makes a hike memorable for them.

The "they lead, you follow" hike

Choose a safe, easy trail and let your toddler set the pace and choose when to stop. Stay within arm's reach near water, edges and roads. You may not get far, but you will see the trail through their eyes.

Games that keep little legs moving

Spotting

I spy colors

"Find something red." Then yellow, then a round thing, then something soft.

Counting

Count the bridges

Count trail markers, bridges, benches or big trees on the way.

Moving

Walk like an animal

Hop like a frog, tiptoe like a fox, stomp like a bear to the next tree.

Goals

The next stop

Small goals work: "Let's walk to that big rock and have a snack there."

Listening

Quiet minute

Stop, close your eyes and count the sounds: birds, wind, water.

Helping

Trail leader

Let them carry a tiny pack with a snack and a toy, and lead to the next marker.

Teach the trail rules early: stay on the path, look but do not pick, never eat berries or plants, and stop when a grown-up says "stop".

The parent pack

Your usual day pack still matters: the hiking basics come first, then the baby and toddler extras on the next page.

Your basics (always)

  • Water for adults and child
  • Snacks and a little extra food
  • Map or downloaded trail map, charged phone
  • Layers and rain jacket for each adult
  • Sun protection: hat, sunglasses, sunscreen
  • First-aid kit (page 17)
  • Headlamp or flashlight
  • Whistle

What changes with a child

  • Two adults? Split it: one carries the child, one the pack
  • One adult? A frame carrier with storage, or a small hip pack
  • Diaper kit in a pocket you can reach
  • Snacks and water you can reach without stopping
  • A small waterproof bag for wet and dirty clothes
  • Spare layer for the child in a top pocket

Weight balance with a carrier

1

Heaviest close to your back

In a frame carrier, keep heavy items low and close to your spine, and do not overload the storage pockets.

2

Know the limit

Frame carriers list a maximum total weight (child plus gear). Weigh your child and the loaded pack at home.

3

Adjust before you go

Fit the hip belt and shoulder straps at home with the child in it, then check again at the trailhead.

4

Test it short

Do a 20 minute walk around the neighborhood with the full load before your first real hike.

The short guide

Dressing for warm days

Little ones overheat and burn faster than adults, and a baby in a carrier is pressed against your warm body. Plan for shade and cool hours.

Timing

Skip the hottest hours

Hike early in the morning or in the evening. Midday sun, roughly 10 am to 4 pm, is the strongest. Pick shady, forested trails and stay near the car on very hot days.

Clothes

Light, loose and covered

Thin, light-colored long sleeves and pants, a wide-brim hat with a neck flap, and breathable fabric. Use the carrier's sun hood.

Sun

Shade first

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises keeping babies under 6 months out of direct sun. Ask your pediatrician about sunscreen for your child's age.

Drinks

Feed and drink often

Offer breast milk or formula often to babies; ask your pediatrician before giving water to a baby under 6 months. Walkers drink small sips at every stop.

Signs your child may be too hot

  • Hot, red or flushed skin, or sweaty hair and neck
  • Fussier than usual, or unusually sleepy
  • Fewer wet diapers than normal
  • Get into shade, loosen clothes, offer drinks
  • Cool with a damp cloth and head back
  • Call your doctor, or 911 in an emergency
Never leave a child in a parked car, not even for a minute at the trailhead. Cars heat up very fast, even on mild days.

Carrier tips for hot days

  • Take your child out of the carrier at every break to cool off
  • A thin cotton cloth between you and your child soaks up sweat
  • Open the carrier's vent panel if it has one
  • Feel the back of the neck: sweaty means too warm

The short guide

Dressing for cold days

You stay warm because you are walking. Your child in the carrier sits still, so they get cold faster than you do.

Layers

Base, warm, shell

A soft base layer (wool or synthetic, not cotton), a fleece or puffy layer, and a wind and water resistant outer layer or bunting suit. One more layer than you wear.

Ends

Head, hands and feet

A warm hat that covers the ears, mittens, warm socks and booties. In a frame carrier the legs dangle, so cover them well.

Check

Feel the back of the neck

Every 15 to 20 minutes, slip a hand to the back of your child's neck or chest. It should feel warm and dry: not cold, and not sweaty.

Timing

Shorter, and in daylight

Days are short in winter. Plan short hikes in the warmest part of the day and be back at the car well before dark.

Head back when

  • The neck or chest feels cool
  • Hands are cold and do not warm up inside mittens
  • Skin looks pale, blotchy or very red
  • Your child is fussy, shivering or unusually quiet
  • Clothes are wet from snow, rain or sweat
  • The wind picks up or it starts to snow hard
Ask your pediatrician how cold is too cold for your child, especially for babies. Bulky snowsuits can interfere with carrier straps and car seat harnesses: take them off in the car and add blankets over the buckled harness instead.

Extra pack items for the cold

  • Spare hat and mittens (they get wet)
  • Warm blanket for stops
  • Warm drink in an insulated bottle
  • Hand warmers for adults only, never against a child's skin

Feeding, snacks and water on the trail

A fed child is a happy hiker. Plan food stops into the route, and bring more than you think you need.

Babies

Nursing and bottles

Pick a few spots on the trail where you can sit comfortably: a bench, a log, a flat rock with a view. A light cover or scarf can help with sun and wind. Bring formula premeasured, and water in an insulated bottle on cold days.

Starting solids

Food they already know

Pouches and soft foods your baby already eats at home. A hike is not the day to try a new food. Pack a bib, a spoon and a bag for the mess.

Toddlers

Small snacks, often

Offer a snack at every stop. Easy, tidy foods your child already eats safely. Cut food to the size your pediatrician recommends for their age.

Adults

Eat and drink too

Carrying a child is hard work. Keep your own snacks and water easy to reach, so you do not skip them while caring for everyone else.

Good habits

  • Clean hands before eating: wipes or sanitizer
  • Sit down to eat, especially toddlers (no snacking while walking or riding in a carrier)
  • Pack out every wrapper, peel and crumb
  • Never feed wildlife, not even birds or squirrels
  • Keep allergy-safe food in its own bag and read every label

Diapers, potty breaks and pack-it-out

Everything you bring in goes home with you. A simple system makes it easy.

Changing

Find a good spot

Change on a pad, off the trail, on durable ground such as rock, gravel or dry grass. Shade in summer, out of the wind in winter.

Pack it out

Diapers and wipes go home

Used diapers and wipes never go in the woods or a cat hole, and are not burned. Seal them in an odor-proof bag, then a second bag, and carry them out.

Potty training

Use restrooms first

Visit the trailhead restroom before you start and when you finish. A travel potty with liners can help; pack the liners out like diapers.

On the trail

When you have to go

Go at least 200 ft (about 60 m, around 70 adult steps) from water, trails and campsites. For poop, dig a cat hole 6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm) deep and cover it.

The diaper kit that lives in one pocket

  • Diapers and wipes
  • Fold-up changing pad
  • Diaper cream
  • Hand sanitizer
  • Odor-proof bags
  • A second bag or dry bag for the trash
  • Small trowel
  • Toilet paper (packed out too)

Why it matters: wipes and diapers do not break down in nature, animals dig them up, and they can pollute water. Leave No Trace principles ask us to pack out all trash, including hygiene products.

Naps on the trail and timing the hike

Many babies and toddlers fall asleep in a carrier to the rhythm of your steps. Plan around naps and a hike can become the best part of the day.

Before
start right after a nap

A well-rested child is happiest at the start. Good for curious walkers.

During
hike through the nap

Some babies nap well in a carrier. You get quiet walking time.

Home
be back before the meltdown

Leave enough time to get home before a big tiredness hits.

Safe sleep in a carrier

  • Keep the face visible and uncovered, and the chin off the chest
  • Support the head with the carrier's hood or head support, as the maker shows
  • Watch for slumping and adjust the straps if the child slides down
  • In a frame carrier, check often that the head is not hanging to one side
  • Do not use a carrier for sleep in place of a safe crib at camp or at home

Bugs, ticks and plants

Most bugs and plants are just part of the fun. A few need care, and a check after every hike takes two minutes.

Mosquitoes

Cover up and add a net

Long sleeves and pants, light colors, and a bug net over the carrier work for babies of every age. Avoid dusk near still water in mosquito season.

Repellents

Read the label for age

Repellents have age limits on their labels: for example, some are not for babies under 2 months, and oil of lemon eucalyptus products are not for children under 3. Ask your pediatrician which to use.

Ticks

Check every hike

Stay in the middle of the trail and away from tall grass. At home, check your child and yourself: hair, behind ears, under arms, waist, behind knees and between toes.

Plants

Leaves of three, let it be

Learn to spot poison ivy and poison oak where you live. Teach toddlers not to touch, pick or taste plants, mushrooms or berries.

If you find a tick

  1. Grasp it with fine-tipped tweezers as close to the skin as you can.
  2. Pull upward with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or squeeze.
  3. Clean the bite and your hands. Note the date and where it was.
  4. Call your pediatrician if a rash or fever appears in the weeks after, or if you could not remove all of it.

Weather and turnaround rules

The most important decision on a family hike is when to turn back. Decide your rules at home, so the choice is easy on the trail.

Weather signs

  • Thunder you can hear, or dark towering clouds
  • Rain or snow starting, wind picking up
  • Temperature dropping fast
  • Fog rolling in

Child signs

  • Crying that a snack, change or cuddle does not fix
  • Too hot, too cold or wet
  • Asking to be carried, again and again
  • Unusually quiet or sleepy

Parent signs

  • Sore back or shoulders from the carrier
  • Low on water, diapers or snacks
  • Losing daylight
  • A feeling that today is not the day
When thunder roars, go indoors. If you hear thunder, lightning is close enough to be a danger. Leave summits, ridges and open areas and head back to the car. Check the forecast the night before and the morning of the hike.

The half-way rule

Turn around when you have used half your energy, half your snacks or half your time, not when you have reached the end of the trail. The way back is often slower with a tired child.

Trail safety with little ones

Toddlers are fast, curious and fearless. A few habits keep everyone safe without spoiling the fun.

Edges

Hold hands near drops

Near any drop-off, steep bank or viewpoint, hold hands or carry. Stay on the uphill side and behind railings.

Water

Arm's reach near water

Creeks, ponds and lakes can be cold, deep and fast. Keep toddlers within arm's reach at all times near water, even shallow water.

Wildlife

Look from a distance

Keep children close and never approach, chase or feed animals. Learn the local advice for bears, moose, snakes or other wildlife before you go.

Together

Stay together

A toddler can vanish around a bend in seconds. Keep them between two adults, or in front of you, and in sight.

Teach "hug a tree"

For preschoolers: if you ever get separated, stop, stay where you are, hug a tree and blow your whistle. Grown-ups will come to you. Practice it as a game at home and give your child a whistle on a short, safe clip on their jacket (not around the neck).

  • Bright clothing so they are easy to see
  • A whistle for each child old enough to use it
  • A recent photo of your child on your phone
  • A note in their pocket with your phone number
  • Someone at home knows your plan
  • Stay on marked trails

First-aid kit additions for kids

Start with your usual hiking first-aid kit, then add a few things for little ones. Ask your pediatrician what medicines and doses are right for your child.

Add for kids

  • Kids' bandages in fun colors (they help)
  • Fine-tipped tweezers for ticks and splinters
  • Child's thermometer
  • Medicines your pediatrician recommends, with the dose written down
  • Any prescribed medicine and allergy plan
  • Saline and gauze for cleaning cuts
  • Instant cold pack
  • Diaper cream and wipes
  • Child's sunscreen and repellent

For you

  • Your own medicines
  • Blister care
  • Elastic bandage
  • Emergency blanket
  • Whistle and headlamp
  • Charged phone and a small power bank

In an emergency

Call 911 (US and Canada). Know the trail name and where you are. If you have no signal, try a text to 911 where available, or move to higher open ground.

Hiking with a little one in each season

Every season is a new trail. Here is what to plan for at a glance.

Spring

Mud, wildflowers, ticks

  • Waterproof boots or rain suit for walkers
  • Changeable weather: pack layers and rain gear
  • Ticks become active: check after every hike
  • Streams run high and cold: stay back from banks
  • Spare socks and a towel in the car
Summer

Heat, sun, bugs

  • Hike early or late, on shady trails
  • Brim hats, light long sleeves, carrier sun hood
  • Drinks at every stop
  • Bug net over the carrier
  • Afternoon thunderstorms: be off exposed ground early
Fall

Leaves, short days, hunting

  • Leaf piles and acorns: the best toddler trail season
  • Shorter days: plan to finish well before sunset
  • Cool mornings and warm afternoons: layers
  • Wear bright colors where hunting is allowed and check hunting seasons
Winter

Cold, snow, ice

  • Short hikes in the warmest hours
  • Neck check every 15 to 20 minutes
  • Traction devices for adults on icy trails
  • A warm drink in an insulated bottle
  • Dry clothes and a warm car waiting

Snow can also be magic: a toddler in a snowsuit on a flat, snowy trail or a short sled pull can be a great winter outing. Keep it short and come back warm.
