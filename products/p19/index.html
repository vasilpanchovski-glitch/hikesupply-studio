How to use this workbook

A long trail is a big dream made of small, plannable pieces. This workbook takes you from "one day" to a start date, one page at a time.

Section hike

A long trail in pieces

You hike the trail a few days or weeks at a time, over months or years. Easier on work and family, and you can pick the best season for each part.

Most useful pages: 5, 6, 8, 9, 17 to 20, 22.

Thru-hike

The whole trail in one go

You walk the whole trail in a single season, usually over weeks or months. It needs more planning: time off, resupply, budget and home logistics.

Use every page, in order, starting a year ahead if you can.

Work through it in four stages

1

Dream and decide

Pages 3 to 6: what you want, an honest fit check, choosing a trail and splitting it into sections.

2

Plan the logistics

Pages 7 to 17: timeline, permits, transport, daily miles, resupply, budget, food and water.

3

Gear, training, safety

Pages 18 to 23: gear weights, a shakedown hike, training, a safety plan and your life at home.

4

On the trail and after

Pages 24 to 31: six weeks at a glance, daily log pages, a town day checklist and your look back.

Printing

Print at Actual size on US Letter. An A4 file and a low-ink printer-friendly file are included. Print the log and table pages as often as you need for your own trips. Use pencil: plans change.

Plan your pace

Daily mileage planner

Start with short days and build up. Most injuries come from doing too much too soon, so plan your first week well below the distance you think you can do.

Hiking day

Your target

A distance you can walk with your full pack, day after day, with time to eat, rest and set up camp before dark.

Zero and nero

Rest days

A zero is a day with no trail miles, usually in town. A nero is a "nearly zero": a short day into or out of town.

Buffer

Spare days

Days for weather, a sore ankle, a closed trail or a slow section. Plans without buffer days tend to break.

Plan your food stops

Resupply planner

Resupply means getting more food and supplies along the way. There are two main ways, and most hikers mix them.

Buy in town

Shop as you go

Flexible and simple: you buy what you feel like eating. Small-town stores can have little choice and higher prices, and some are a long hitch or walk from the trail.

Mail drop

Send boxes ahead

Good for places with no store, special diets and gear swaps. Needs a helper at home or careful packing before you leave, and you must reach the post office while it is open.

General Delivery address (USPS)

Your full legal name
c/o General Delivery
Town, State ZIP
Please hold for hiker. ETA: date

General Delivery is a USPS service, so use the Postal Service, not other carriers. Mail is held for a limited time (often up to 30 days). Bring photo ID to collect it. In Canada, ask Canada Post about General Delivery: hold times differ.

Before you rely on a stop

  • Call the post office: small offices can have short hours and close on weekends
  • Some hostels and outfitters hold packages for hikers; ask first, some charge a fee
  • Check how far the town is from the trail and how you get there
  • Carry enough food to reach the next stop, plus a spare day

Packing tips

Food planner

Appetite often grows a lot after the first week or two on a long trail. Plan by weight and adjust at every resupply.

1.5–2.5 lb
(0.7–1.1 kg) food per person per day

A common planning range for backpacking. Many long-distance hikers end up at the top of it or above.

A handy target for trail food. Nuts, nut butters and oils are higher; fresh food is much lower.

Water planner

Know your next
source

Water is heavy: 1 liter weighs about 2.2 lb (1 kg). Carry enough to reach the next reliable source, plus a margin for heat and climbs. Treat all water from streams, lakes and springs.

Carry capacity liters

Treatment options

  • Filter: squeeze, pump or gravity
  • Chemical: tablets or drops; follow the wait time
  • UV light pen: works best with clear water
  • Boiling: needs fuel and time

Follow the instructions of your filter or treatment exactly, and protect filters from freezing. In dry areas, never count on a water cache: they can be empty. Check recent water reports from the trail organization or land manager.

Training plan link-up

8 weeks to
trail-ready legs

You do not need to be an athlete to start a long trail, but strong legs and tough feet make the first weeks much nicer. This is a simple outline. For a full day-by-day plan, see our Trail Ready 8-week training plan (optional).

Good habits

  • Train in the shoes and socks you will hike in
  • Build up slowly; rest when something hurts
  • Hills and stairs are the best practice for climbs
  • Practice with the pack you will carry
Check with your doctor before starting a new training plan, especially if you have a health condition, an old injury, or have not been active for a while. Stop and get help for chest pain, dizziness or unusual shortness of breath.
