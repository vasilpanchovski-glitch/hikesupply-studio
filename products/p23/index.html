How to use this guide

Joshua Tree is a big, quiet desert park with no gas, no food and no drinking water inside its borders. A little planning turns that from a worry into part of the adventure. This guide walks you through it.

Who it is for

First-time visitors and beginner to intermediate hikers. Every hike here is a day hike on a marked trail, from a 15-minute walk among cactus to a half-day trek to a palm oasis.

Distances are round trip, in miles and kilometers. Climbs are total elevation gain, in feet and meters. Times are the park's own estimates. All trail figures come from the National Park Service.

The park

At a glance

Two deserts meet inside the park. The higher, cooler Mojave Desert in the west and north is where the Joshua trees and giant boulder piles are. The lower, hotter Colorado Desert in the south and east has cholla, ocotillo and wide open basins.

How far is it? (check your route)

Roughly 1 hour by car from Palm Springs, about 2.5 to 3 hours from Los Angeles, about 3 hours from San Diego, and about 4 hours from Phoenix to the Cottonwood entrance, without traffic. Weekend and holiday traffic can add a lot.

Three things that surprise first-timers

There is no water, gas or food inside the park, so you bring it all. Driving from the West Entrance to Cottonwood Spring takes about 1 hour 20 minutes without stops. And desert nights can be near freezing even when the day was warm.

Timing

When to go: season by season

October to April is the main season, when days are mild enough for hiking. That is also when everyone else comes, so weekends and holidays are busy. Summer is for early mornings and starry nights, not long hikes.

Dec to FebCool days, highs around 60 °F (15 °C), nights near or below freezing. Snow now and then up high. Short days: plan around sunset.
MarchComfortable and busy. Spring break crowds from March to mid-April. Wildflowers in years with good winter rain.
AprilWarm days, still busy, especially at Easter. Heat starts to build late in the month.
MayGetting hot. Hike early. Last busy weekends before summer.
Jun to AugHighs around 100 °F (38 °C), nights near 75 °F (24 °C). Hike only at dawn. Some campgrounds and 49 Palms Oasis close. Monsoon storms mid-July to early September.
SeptemberStill hot. Flash flood risk from late summer storms. Few visitors.
Oct to NovThe season starts again: highs of 70 to 85 °F (21 to 29 °C), cool nights, busy weekends. Campfire ban ends October 1.

Why it gets so busy

The park says to expect long lines, limited parking, busy trails and full campgrounds on holidays, on weekends from October to May, and during spring break. A weekday visit, or arriving at the gate before 8 am, makes a big difference.

Buy it before you arrive

Passes are sold on recreation.gov at no extra cost. Print it or save it to your phone before you drive in, because there is little or no signal at the gate. Buying ahead also gets you through the entrance line faster.

Paying at the park

The North and West entrance stations take cards only, no cash. Visitor centers take cash or card. The fee booth is sometimes closed: you may be asked to show your pass on the way out, so keep it handy.

Fee-free days

A few days a year, entry is free for US citizens and residents. The 2026 dates have passed. Check nps.gov/jotr for the next year's dates, and expect those days to be crowded.

Visiting from Canada or abroad?

The non-resident America the Beautiful pass is $250. For a single visit to Joshua Tree, the $30 vehicle pass is enough. Prices change: check nps.gov/jotr/planyourvisit/fees.htm before you buy.

Parking fills early

On busy days the small lots at Hidden Valley, Barker Dam, Ryan Mountain and Skull Rock fill by mid-morning. Arrive early, have a second hike in mind, and never park on plants or off the pavement.

Dirt roads

Some roads, like Geology Tour Road, are unpaved, sandy or rough. Ask a ranger before you try one.

Never drive into a flooded dip or wash. Water on the road can be deeper and faster than it looks. Wait, or turn around.

1 day in the park

1 day: Joshua trees and bouldersWest in, north out
Before 8
Buy your pass online, fill up with water and gas in Joshua Tree, and enter early to get a parking spot.
Morning
Hidden Valley loop (hike 3), then the Barker Dam loop (hike 4). Watch for bighorn sheep near the water.
Midday
Picnic lunch in the shade at a picnic area. Cap Rock for a short, easy walk among the Joshua trees.
Afternoon
Drive to Keys View (hike 2) for the big view, then Skull Rock (hike 6) on the way east.
Sunset
Stay for golden light on the rocks, then stars at a designated stargazing lot (page 13) before driving out.

Coming from Phoenix or the I-10?

Enter at Cottonwood instead. Walk to Cottonwood Spring, hike Mastodon Peak (hike 8) in the cool morning, then drive north through the Cholla Cactus Garden (hike 1) and leave by the North Entrance after Skull Rock at sunset.

Rules of thumb

  • Start early, and do your longest walk first while it is cool
  • Carry at least a gallon (3.8 L) of water per person for the day
  • Keep half a tank of gas, there is none in the park
  • Plan your last hike to finish before dark
  • Have a second hike in mind if a parking lot is full

Pace yourself

Sun, dry air and wind tire you out faster than you expect. Keep the middle of each day easy, drink before you are thirsty, and swap a long hike for a short walk if anyone feels unwell.

Summer rule from the park

In hot months, start your activity before 10 am or after 4 pm, take shade breaks, and turn back early. The strenuous hikes in this guide are not for hot days at all.

Rain and storms

Summer storms (mid-July to early September) can cause flash floods, even where it is sunny. Stay out of washes and canyons when storms are around, and never drive through water.

Joshua Tree National Park12
After dark

Stargazing

Joshua Tree is an International Dark Sky Park. On a clear, moonless night you can see the Milky Way from a parking lot. Light from nearby towns still glows on some horizons, so the darkest skies are in the south and east.

Designated stargazing lots

Easiest

Quail Springs, Hidden Valley, Cap Rock and Ryan Mountain parking areas.

Pinto Basin Road

Darkest by road

Pullouts between the Cholla Cactus Garden and Cottonwood have the least traffic and the darkest skies.

Cottonwood Campground

Darkest campground

The darkest skies of any park campground. Camping fees apply.

Your campsite

No driving needed

Jumbo Rocks, Ryan and Belle are all good, away from town lights.

Make it a good night

  • Check the moon: moonless nights are best
  • Use red light only. Eyes take 20 to 30 minutes to adjust
  • Bring warm layers: it cools fast after sunset
  • A folding chair and a blanket
  • Snacks and water

Stay safe in the dark

  • Stay awake and within 20 ft (6 m) of your car at lots and pullouts
  • Closed-toe shoes: cactus and uneven ground
  • Park only in lots and pullouts
  • No sleeping in cars on roadsides or lots
  • Day-use areas close from dusk to dawn

Make your own red light

Tape a piece of red cellophane or a red plastic bag over a flashlight. A white phone screen undoes your night vision in seconds, so turn the brightness right down.

Joshua Tree National Park19
On the rocks

Rock scrambling and climbing

Joshua Tree's boulder piles are world famous with climbers, and almost everyone wants to scramble up a few. Done with care, it is the most fun you will have in the park. Done carelessly, it is the most common way people get hurt.

Do

Scramble smart

  • Only climb up what you can easily climb back down
  • Going down is harder than going up: test it early
  • Wear shoes with sticky soles, not sandals
  • Keep children within arm's reach on rocks
  • Stay off rocks when they are wet or icy
Know your limits

Real climbing

  • Roped climbing needs gear and training
  • Want to try? Book a guide service permitted by the park
  • Some areas close to climbing to protect wildlife and cultural sites
  • Check the park's climbing closures before you go
  • Never climb on rock art or historic structures

Look after the living soil

Between the rocks you will see a dark, lumpy crust on the sand. This is living soil: tiny organisms that hold the desert together and help plants grow. One footstep can crush it, and it takes many years to recover. Stay on trails, rock or sandy washes.

Plants are fragile too

Do not climb on Joshua trees or break their branches. Walk around shrubs, not through them, and leave flowers and seed pods where they are.

Getting lost in the boulders

Boulder piles all look alike. Note landmarks, look back often, and stay on marked trails. The park warns that some routes on apps are not real trails.

Falls are serious here: the nearest hospital is a long drive away and rescues take time. If you are not sure you can get down, do not go up.

Heat

Drink when thirsty and eat salty snacks. Dizzy, weak or sweating heavily? Stop, rest in shade and drink, and get medical help if it gets worse. In summer, hike only before 10 am or after 4 pm.

Flash floods

They can strike suddenly, even when it is sunny where you are. Stay out of washes when storms are around.

Tell someone your plan

Leave your route and return time with someone at home. Bring a second way to call for help, like a satellite messenger.

Emergency

Call 911, or park dispatch 909-383-5651. No signal? Move to higher ground or drive toward a town. Pick a turnaround time before you start.

Joshua Tree National Park21
Nature

Wildlife and plants

Most desert animals are shy and come out at dawn, dusk and night. A few plants and animals need respect. None of them are a reason to stay home.

Cholla cactus

Watch for

Spiny segments that break off at a touch and stick to skin, shoes and dogs.

Do not pull with fingers. Lift it off with a comb or tweezers, then remove the spines one by one.

Rattlesnakes

Watch for

Most active in warm months, often at dusk. They rest under rocks and bushes.

Watch where you put hands and feet. Back away slowly. If bitten, walk calmly to your car and go to the nearest hospital.

Bees

Watch for

Drawn to water and food in summer, at Jumbo Rocks, Keys View and the Cholla Cactus Garden.

Move away calmly, do not swat. Keep drinks covered. Bring your allergy medicine if you need it.

Scorpions

They hide under rocks and come out on warm nights. Shake out shoes, clothes and sleeping bags in the morning, and use a light when walking in camp at night.

Desert tortoise

A federally threatened species. If you see one, enjoy it from a distance and never pick it up: a frightened tortoise can lose water it needs to survive. Check under your car before driving off.

Bighorn sheep, coyotes, birds

Watch from a distance and never feed any animal. Fed coyotes become bold and often have to be removed. Keep food packed away in your car or a closed container.

Leave No Trace

Pack out every scrap, including fruit peels. Take only photos: rocks, plants and old mining objects stay where they are.

First aid tip

Pack tweezers and a fine comb with your first aid kit. They are the most useful tools in the park.

Dress for a 40 degree swing

A desert day can start near freezing and reach the 80s °F (high 20s °C) by afternoon, then drop fast after sunset. Start in layers, take them off as you warm up, and keep a warm layer in your pack, even for short walks.

Joshua Tree National Park23
With kids and dogs

With kids and with dogs

Boulders to scramble, a skull-shaped rock and a cactus garden make Joshua Tree a great park for kids. Dogs have a harder time here: the rules are strict, for good reasons.

Junior Ranger program

Free activity booklets at the visitor centers. Finish the activities, then go back to a ranger for a badge.

Best walks with kids

  • Cap Rock: flat, 0.4 mi (0.6 km) loop
  • Hidden Valley (hike 3)
  • Barker Dam (hike 4)
  • Discovery Trail and Skull Rock (hike 6)
  • Arch Rock (hike 5)
  • Oasis of Mara at the Twentynine Palms visitor center

Kids in the desert

Children heat up and dry out faster than adults and may not say so. Give them their own water bottle, plan shady breaks, and turn back at the first signs of crankiness, headache or tiredness.

The rules for dogs

  • Dogs are not allowed on any trail, except the paved Oasis of Mara walk
  • Always on a leash, and within 100 ft (30 m) of a road
  • Allowed in campgrounds and picnic areas
  • Never left alone, and never in a hot car

Why the strict rules

Hot sand and rock can burn paws, cholla spines stick to fur and mouths, and dogs can disturb wildlife and the fragile soil. If your plan is mostly hiking, think about boarding your dog in town for the day.

Service animals

Trained service animals are allowed. Check the current pet and service animal rules on nps.gov/jotr before you go.

Hold hands on high rocks. Boulder tops are often higher than they look, and coming down is the tricky part.

Ways to save

One $30 vehicle pass covers a whole car for 7 days. First-come campgrounds cost less than reservable ones. Buying water by the gallon in town costs far less than small bottles.
