How to use this passport

State and provincial parks are the parks most of us visit most often: close to home, open year-round, and full of good trails. This passport turns every visit into a page worth keeping.

1

Print what you need

Print at Actual size on US Letter. Normal printer paper works fine. Light card stock (about 65 lb / 176 gsm) holds ink stamps and stickers better. An A4 file and a low-ink printer-friendly file are included.

2

Keep it light

You do not need all 40 pages in your pack. Take only the visit logs for this trip and add them to a binder or folder at home. Reprint any page for your own use as often as you like.

3

Color and check off

Color each state on the map (page 5) and each province or territory (page 6) the first time you visit one of its parks. Tick it on the master checklist (pages 7 to 9).

4

Log and stamp

Fill in a visit log (pages 10 to 33) for each park day. The stamp boxes are left empty on purpose, so a park stamp, a sticker or a ticket stub fits cleanly. More blank boxes on pages 34 and 35.

Where do park stamps come from?

Many state and provincial parks keep a stamp at the park office, visitor center or nature center, and several states run their own passport programs with booklets and prizes. Nothing is guaranteed at every park, so ask at the office when you arrive. No stamp? Write the park name in the box, add a sketch, or glue in your day-use receipt.

Checked November 2026. Every agency name and website in this passport was checked on the agency's own site in November 2026. Fees, hours, reservations, pet rules and closures change often. Always check the park's official page before you go.

Good to know

State, provincial and national parks: who runs what

Three kinds of parks, three different owners. That is why a pass for one rarely works at the others.

US national parks

Run by the National Park Service (federal)

64 national parks plus hundreds of other NPS sites. One pass, the America the Beautiful pass, covers entrance fees at all of them.

It does not cover state parks.

US state parks

Run by each state

Every state has its own parks agency. Some are part of a natural resources department, some share an agency with fish and wildlife, some stand alone.

Each state sets its own fees, passes and rules.

Canadian provincial parks

Run by each province or territory

Each province and territory runs its own parks. Canada's national parks are run by Parks Canada, a separate federal agency.

The Parks Canada Discovery Pass does not cover provincial parks.

A name that confuses many visitors

Québec's "parcs nationaux" (Québec national parks) are provincial parks run by Sépaq for the province of Québec. They are not part of Parks Canada, so they have their own entry fees and their own annual card.

What usually differs from state to state

  • Day-use or parking fee, and whether it is per car or per person
  • Different prices for residents and out-of-state cars
  • Annual passes and passport programs
  • Campsite reservations and how far ahead they open
  • Pet rules: most parks want pets on a leash, and some trails and beaches allow none
  • Opening hours, seasonal closures and gate times
  • Firewood rules: buy it where you will burn it
  • Drones, swimming areas and hunting seasons

Each state's and province's official parks website is on pages 7 to 9. It is the only place to trust for today's rules.

Crossing the border for a park day?

  • Passport or other accepted travel document for everyone in the car
  • Check what food you can bring across: fresh fruit, meat and firewood are often restricted
  • Pets need their own papers, such as a rabies certificate
  • Distances in Canada are in km (1 mile = 1.6 km)
  • Check your phone plan for roaming
  • Check the official government border pages before you go

How to find a park's rules in two minutes

Open the official website above and search for the park by name. On its page, look for alerts or notices first (closures, fire, flooding), then fees and hours, then pet rules and reservations. Websites move: if a link does not work, search the agency name plus "state parks" or "provincial parks" and use the result that ends in the government's own domain.

Some states run their parks inside a larger department (natural resources, or fish and wildlife), so the website may open on the department's home page. Look for the parks section.

Annual passes and passport programs

Many states and provinces sell an annual pass or a vehicle permit, and some run passport programs with stamps and rewards. If you visit the same state's parks three or four times a year, a pass often pays for itself. Prices and rules change, so fill this in from the official websites on pages 7 to 9.

One page to remember

Leave No Trace and park etiquette

State parks are busy, shared places close to town. These seven habits keep them good for everyone.

1

Plan ahead and prepare

Check the park's page for hours, fees and closures. Carry water, food, a layer, a light and a map.

2

Travel on durable surfaces

Stay on the marked trail, even through mud, so it does not widen. Never cut switchbacks.

3

Dispose of waste properly

Pack out everything you bring, food scraps too. Use restrooms where there are some.

4

Leave what you find

Flowers, rocks, shells and old artifacts stay where they are for the next visitor.

5

Minimize campfire impacts

Use existing fire rings only, follow any fire ban, and buy firewood where you burn it.

6

Respect wildlife

Watch from a distance, never feed animals, and store food so animals cannot reach it.

7

Be considerate of others

Yield to hikers going uphill, keep pets on a leash where required, and keep music off.

Each park has its own rules

Pets, drones, swimming, collecting and hunting seasons all have rules that change from state to state and park to park. In fall, some parks allow hunting: check the park page and wear bright colors. When in doubt, ask at the park office.

Pack these on every park day hike

  • Water, more than you think
  • Snacks or lunch
  • Rain shell and a warm layer
  • Map, offline or on paper
  • Headlamp or flashlight
  • Sun hat and sunscreen
  • Small first aid kit
  • Tell someone your plan and return time

Based on the seven Leave No Trace principles. Learn more at lnt.org.
