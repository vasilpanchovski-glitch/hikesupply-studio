How to use this planner

This is your plan for a year outside. It works for a first walk in the woods and for a big summit, alone, with friends or with the family. Print it, keep it on the fridge or in a binder, and fill it in as you go.

1

Set your goals

Page 4. Pick a distance in miles or kilometers, a few new trails, one big hike and one skill. Small and steady beats big and forgotten.

2

See the year

Page 5 shows all of 2027 with the long weekends in the US and Canada. Circle the weekends you want to keep free.

3

Plan each month

Pages 6 to 17: a calendar, space for the hikes you plan, and a tip for that season. Write hikes on the calendar in pencil, and in pen once they are done.

4

Take the challenge

Page 18: one box for every week. Any walk outside on a trail counts. Miss a week? Just keep going.

5

Color it in

Pages 19 to 21: color one space for every mile, every kilometer or every summit and viewpoint you reach.

6

Look back

Pages 25 and 26: a short review every three months and a year in review in December. Use it to set next year's goals.

Pick your units

Every distance has room for miles or kilometers. Use one for the whole year so your totals add up. 1 mile = 1.61 km, 1 km = 0.62 miles, 1,000 ft = 305 m.

Seasonal tips are general. Conditions change from region to region and week to week: check the park or forest website and the weather before every hike, and turn back when in doubt.

Mud season

Many trails turn to mud as the ground thaws. Walk through the middle of the mud, not around it, so the trail does not get wider. Clocks spring forward on March 14 in most of the US and Canada.

Season tip

Snow up high

Snow can stay on high and north-facing trails into early summer. Read recent trail reports, and turn back at snow you are not equipped for. Snowmelt makes streams fast and cold: if a crossing looks deep or fast, do not cross.

No federal holidays this month. A good month for a quiet weekday hike.

Before you plan

Dates to look up for 2027

Some of the most useful dates for hikers are announced by the parks themselves, often only weeks or months ahead. We did not guess them here. Look them up and write them in.

Fee-free days

nps.gov

The US National Park Service announces its fee-free entrance days each year. Check who they apply to and which parks charge fees at all.

Parks Canada

parks.canada.ca

Reservation launch dates for campgrounds and backcountry are set park by park. Popular sites can fill on the first morning.

Reservations and timed entry

recreation.gov

Some busy parks need a timed entry or vehicle reservation in season, and many campgrounds open months ahead. Rules change from year to year.

Permits and lotteries

Your park or forest website

Famous day hikes and backcountry trips can need a permit or a lottery entry, with deadlines months before the hike.

DateWhatWhere to book or checkDone

State and provincial parks have their own fees, passes and booking dates. Always check the official website before you go.
