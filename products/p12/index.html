How to use this planner

A good camping trip is mostly decided at home: the right campground, a booked site, food that stays cold and a car you can unpack in the right order. This planner walks you through it, one page at a time.

This planner is for you if

  • You camp at campgrounds, by car, with a tent
  • You are new to camping, or camping with kids for the first time
  • You camp in US or Canadian parks and forests
  • You want lists you can tick off, not a pile of notes

What is inside

  • Pages 3 to 5: choose, book and pick a site
  • Pages 6 and 7: trip overview and weather plan
  • Pages 8 to 12: packing lists and car order
  • Pages 13 to 16: meals, groceries and the cooler
  • Pages 17 to 21: camping safely and well
  • Pages 22 and 23: a log for 8 campsites

Printing tips

Print the whole planner once, then reprint the checklists, meal planner and grocery list for every trip. It prints on US Letter or A4, and a low-ink printer-friendly version is included. For the campsite log, a heavier paper or light cardstock holds up better at the picnic table.

Plan

Choosing a campground

Campgrounds differ a lot, from busy parks with hot showers to quiet forest loops with a vault toilet and a water pump. Pick the kind that fits your group.

National parksBig scenery, ranger programs and trails nearby. Popular campgrounds fill early, so plan ahead. In the US many are booked on recreation.gov; in Canada through the Parks Canada reservation service.
National forests and BLM land (US)Often quieter and simpler, sometimes with first-come, first-served sites. Facilities can be basic: check for drinking water before you go.
State and provincial parksFamily-friendly, often with flush toilets, showers, beaches and playgrounds. Each state and province has its own booking site.
Private campgroundsThe most comforts: showers, laundry, camp stores, sometimes pools and Wi-Fi. A good choice for a first night out with kids.

What to look for

  • Drinking water at the campground, or bring your own
  • Toilets: flush or vault
  • Showers, if your group needs them
  • Tent pads and the largest tent size allowed
  • Distance to the trails or lake you came for
  • Shade, especially for summer trips
  • Pets allowed, and the leash rules
  • Generator hours and quiet hours
  • Cell signal, if you need it (often none)
  • Elevation: higher campgrounds are usually colder at night

Read the recent reviews and the official page

Reviews tell you about noise, bugs and the best loops. The official park page tells you what is true today: closures, water outages, fire restrictions and bear rules.

Booking your site

Popular campgrounds can fill within minutes of opening, especially summer weekends. Knowing where and when to book is half the job.

US federal campgroundsNational parks, national forests, BLM and Army Corps of Engineers campgrounds mostly book on recreation.gov. Many open on a rolling window a set number of months ahead. The window differs by campground, so check its page.
Parks CanadaBook at reservation.pc.gc.ca. Each park opens its season's reservations on a launch date, usually announced in winter. Note the date and time zone and be ready when it opens.
State and provincial parksEach has its own booking site and rules. Booking windows and cancellation fees vary a lot from one to the next.
First-come, first-servedSome sites cannot be booked. Arrive early, ideally midweek or in the morning, and follow the posted way to claim and pay. Always have a backup campground in mind.

Before you click "book"

  • The site fits your tent (pad size) and your car
  • Number of people and vehicles allowed
  • Check-in and check-out times
  • Cancellation and change rules
  • Pets, fires and firewood rules
  • Walk-in or drive-in site
  • Water, toilet and shower distance
  • Accessible sites, if you need one
  • Save the confirmation (photo or print)
  • Add the booking to page 6
Use only the official booking site. Search results and ads sometimes lead to look-alike sites that charge extra fees. Check the web address before you pay.
Camping Trip Planner4
On arrival

Picking a good site

If you choose your site on arrival, or pick it from a map when booking, a few minutes of looking saves a bad night.

1

Look up first

Check for dead trees and broken, hanging branches over the tent pad and parking spot. Wind can bring them down. If you see one, choose another spot.

2

Flat, high and dry

Pitch on the tent pad or flat ground, not in a dip where rain pools. Lie down on the spot before you pitch: you will feel any slope or rock.

3

Wind and sun

Turn the tent door away from the wind. Trees on the east side give shade on a hot morning; open ground to the east warms a cold one.

4

Close, but not too close

Near the toilets and water is handy at night, but right next to them means foot traffic and lights. Two or three sites away is a good balance.

A quick site check

  • No dead trees or hanging branches above
  • Tent pad is flat and drains well
  • Away from ant hills and wasp nests
  • Fire ring is clear of branches overhead
  • Not on a path to the water or toilets
  • Picnic table and food storage nearby
  • Within the site's marked boundary
  • No standing water nearby (mosquitoes)

Stay on the site

Pitch tents only where the campground allows, usually the tent pad. It protects plants and roots, and it keeps you where the ground is prepared for it.

If it rains

  • Rainfly on and guylines tight, tent pad clear of puddles
  • Tarp over the picnic table, angled so water runs off
  • Wet clothes in a bag, dry clothes in another
  • Rainy-day ideas on page 21

If there is thunder

  • A tent does not protect you from lightning
  • Go to a hard-topped car with the windows up, or a solid building
  • Stay there until 30 minutes after the last thunder
  • Away from lone tall trees, water and open high ground

Cold nights

  • Sleeping bag rated for the night low, and a warm pad under you
  • Dry base layers and a hat for sleeping
  • A warm drink and a snack before bed

Hot days

  • Shade over the table, cooler in the shade
  • Plenty of water, a refill plan
  • Rest at midday, hike early
Backup campground or plan if ours is closed (fire, flood, storm)

  • Can opener
  • Food thermometer
  • Foil and oven mitt

Eating

  • Plates, bowls and mugs
  • Forks, spoons and knives
  • Reusable water bottles
  • Tablecloth and clips

Food and water

  • Cooler and ice (page 16)
  • Water jug with a tap
  • Food storage bins with lids
  • Zip bags and containers
  • Coffee or tea kit

Cleaning

  • Two wash tubs: wash and rinse
  • Biodegradable soap and sponge
  • Dish towel and paper towels
  • Trash and recycling bags
  • Hand soap or sanitizer

Wash up the right way

Use the campground's dish station or gray-water drain if it has one, never the drinking water tap. Scrape food scraps into the trash first. Keep soap and dishwater away from lakes and streams.

Light the stove once at home before the trip and check you have enough fuel, plus a spare.

Kids and family add-on

Camping with kids works best when they have a job, a light and something to do when the adults are busy.

Gear for kids

  • Their own headlamp or flashlight
  • A whistle on a cord
  • Bright jacket or shirt, easy to spot
  • Extra full set of clothes
  • Comfort item for bedtime
  • Kid-size camp chair

Little ones

  • Diapers, wipes and a sealed bag
  • Portable crib or play yard
  • Baby carrier for walks
  • Night light for the tent

Things to do

  • Cards and a small board game
  • Nature journal and pencils
  • Magnifying glass
  • Ball or frisbee
  • Campsite bingo (page 21)

Camp jobs for kids

  • Collect trash around the site
  • Fill the water jug (with an adult)
  • Set the table
  • Hand out the tent stakes
  • Check the site before leaving

The family safety talk

Before anyone explores, agree on the boundaries of your site. Teach kids: if you are lost, stay where you are, hug a tree and blow your whistle three times. An adult will come to you. Keep kids away from the fire ring, the stove and the road through the campground.

Camping Trip Planner11
Checklist

Packing the car

Pack in reverse: what you need first at camp goes in last, so it comes out first. You set up faster, even if you arrive in the dark or the rain.

1
In firstdeepest, at the back

Things for the last day and the drive home: dry clothes bag, games and extras you will not need right away.

2
Bulky and heavylow, against the seat back

Kitchen bins, water jug, camp chairs and sleeping bags. Heavy things low and forward keep the car stable.

3
Shelternear the door

Tent, footprint, stakes, mallet and tarp. The first thing you unload, so the tent is up before anything else.

4
Coolerlast in, easy to reach

In the shade inside the car, not in a hot roof box. Reach it without unpacking everything.

5
In the cabinwith you

Headlamps, rain jackets, snacks, water, first aid kit, the printed booking and this planner.

  • Nothing loose that can fly forward in a hard stop
  • Windows and mirrors kept clear
  • Roof box within the car's load limit
  • Spare fuel canisters upright, away from heat
  • Tire pressure and spare checked
  • Gas tank full before the last town
  • Phone charger and offline map
  • Trash bag for the drive

A first-hour bag

Pack one bag with what you need in the first hour at camp: headlamps, a lighter, the kettle and a hot drink, snacks, rain jackets and the tent repair kit. Arriving late or in the rain is easy when it is right by the door.

Camping Trip Planner13
Food

Easy camp meal ideas

Camp food should be simple, filling and quick to clean up. Mix and match these ideas to fill your meal planner.

Breakfast

Oatmeal with fruit and nuts. Breakfast burritos with eggs scrambled at home and carried in a sealed container. Pancakes from a shake-and-pour mix. Yogurt and granola for the first morning.

One-pot

Pasta with sauce and vegetables. Chili from canned beans. Rice and beans with salsa. Soup with bread. Fewer pots means less washing up.

Foil packs

Chopped potatoes, vegetables and sausage, or fish with lemon, sealed in foil and cooked on hot coals, not flames. Turn them halfway.

No-cook

Wraps with hummus, cheese and vegetables. Crackers, cheese and fruit. Peanut butter sandwiches. Good for arrival day or a rainy evening.

Make it easier

  • Chop vegetables and mix spices at home
  • Pack each meal's ingredients in one bag
  • Freeze one dinner flat; it doubles as ice
  • Bring a backup no-cook meal
  • Cook before dark when you can
  • Make a little extra hot water for washing up
Cook meat all the way through. Use a food thermometer: 165°F (74°C) for poultry and 160°F (71°C) for ground beef and pork. Keep raw meat away from ready-to-eat food, and use a separate board and utensils.

Camping Trip Planner15
Food

Cooler packing and food safety

Cold food is safe food. Keep everything in the cooler at 40°F (4°C) or below: a small fridge thermometer in the cooler tells you at a glance.

1
Bottomcoldest

Frozen raw meat, sealed in leak-proof bags or containers, plus block ice or frozen water bottles.

2
Middlecold

Dairy, eggs, leftovers and the next day's meals, with ice packed around them.

3
Topused first

Fruit, vegetables, sandwiches and anything you reach for often.

Keep it cold longer

  • Pre-chill the cooler the night before
  • Pack food already cold or frozen
  • Block ice lasts longer than cubes
  • Drinks in a second cooler
  • Cooler in the shade, lid closed

Keep it safe

  • Seal food so meltwater cannot get in
  • Raw meat low and sealed, never above other food
  • Wash hands before handling food
  • Toss food that has warmed up
  • When in doubt, throw it out

The two-hour rule

Perishable food should not sit out of the cooler for more than two hours, or one hour when it is above 90°F (32°C). Put food back as soon as the meal is served.

In bear country the cooler counts as food: it goes in the bear locker or wherever the posted rules say (page 17).

Camping Trip Planner16
Camp safely

Food storage and wildlife

Food left out teaches animals to come back for more. A fed bear or raccoon becomes a problem for the next campers, and for the animal itself.

1

Nothing scented in the tent

No food, snacks, drinks, toothpaste, deodorant or sunscreen in the tent. Wrappers in a pocket count too.

2

Use the locker

Where the campground has a bear locker, store all food, coolers, cooking gear and toiletries in it, and latch it every time.

3

Follow the posted rules

Some campgrounds allow food in a closed, hard-sided vehicle; some do not. The sign at the campground or the ranger has the answer.

4

Clean as you go

Never leave food or a cooler out unattended, even for a few minutes. Wipe the table, take trash to the bins, and never burn food scraps in the fire.

Never feed wildlife, not even a chipmunk. Human food makes animals sick and bolder.

In bear country: learn the park's bear advice before you go and follow it. Carry bear spray if the land manager recommends it and know how to use it. Keep a clean camp and stay aware at dawn and dusk.

The usual camp visitors

RaccoonsClever with lids and zips. Use latched bins or the locker, never a soft cooler bag left out at night.
Squirrels and chipmunksChew through bags and tents for crumbs. Keep snacks sealed and out of reach, even during the day.

Food storage rules are set by each park and campground, and in some areas they come with fines. Read them on arrival.

Camping Trip Planner17
Camp safely

Campfire and stove safety

A campfire is the heart of the evening. It also starts many wildfires. Check the rules every trip: fire bans can start or change at short notice.

Before you light

  • Check fire restrictions for today
  • Use the existing fire ring only
  • Clear branches and gear from around it
  • Water bucket and shovel at hand
  • Local or certified heat-treated firewood only (moved wood spreads tree pests)

While it burns

  • Keep it small and low
  • Never leave it unattended, not even briefly
  • Kids and pets well back from the ring
  • No gas or lighter fluid on a fire
  • Put it out if the wind picks up

Putting it out: drown, stir, feel

Drown

Pour plenty of water on the fire, until the hissing stops.

Stir

Stir the ashes and embers with a shovel, then add more water.

Feel

Hold the back of your hand close. If it is too hot to touch, it is too hot to leave.

Never use a stove, grill, heater or fuel lantern inside a tent, camper or closed car. They give off carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell, which can kill. They are also a fire risk. Cook and heat outdoors only.

Camp stove basics

  • Flat, stable surface, outdoors
  • Well away from the tent and dry grass
  • Never leave a lit stove alone
  • Let it cool before changing fuel
  • Store fuel away from the fire
  • Check hoses and seals each trip
Camping Trip Planner18
Camp well

Leave No Trace at the campsite

Good campers leave the site better than they found it, and remember that the neighbors came for the quiet, too.

The seven principles, at the campground

  • Plan ahead and prepare: book, check the rules and pack the right gear
  • Travel and camp on durable surfaces: tents on the pad, paths not shortcuts
  • Dispose of waste properly: pack out trash, wash dishes at the station
  • Leave what you find: no cutting branches, no carving, no moving rocks
  • Minimize campfire impacts: existing rings, small fires, out cold
  • Respect wildlife: store food, never feed animals
  • Be considerate of other visitors: quiet hours, lights, space

Quiet hours

Most campgrounds have quiet hours at night, often from about 10 pm, and generator hours. Check the posted times. Voices and music carry far at night.

Good neighbor habits

Walk around other sites, not through them. Point lanterns down. Keep dogs leashed and quiet. Say hello: neighbors are your best help in camp.

Camping with a dog

Check that pets are allowed before you book. Keep your dog leashed, never leave it alone at the site or in the car, and store its food like your own. Bag and bin every poop.

The seven principles are from the Leave No Trace Center for Outdoor Ethics. Learn more at lnt.org.

Camping Trip Planner19
Checklist

Arrive and set up, pack up and leave

When you arrive

  • Check in, or claim and pay for the site
  • Read the board: rules, fire level, wildlife
  • Walk the site: look up, check the ground
  • Pitch the tent first, rainfly on
  • Tarp over the table if rain is possible
  • Kitchen set up, cooler in the shade
  • Food storage sorted (locker or car)
  • Find the toilets, water and trash bins
  • Headlamps out before dark
  • Safety talk with the kids

When you leave

  • Fire out cold: drown, stir, feel
  • All trash and recycling to the bins
  • Walk the site for small trash
  • Tent shaken out, stakes counted
  • Coolers drained only where allowed
  • Nothing left in the bear locker
  • Fire ring and table left clean
  • Check out on time
  • Last look: under the table and car
  • Note how it went on page 22

Packing a wet tent

If you must pack the tent wet, set it up or hang it to dry as soon as you get home, within a day or two. A tent stored damp grows mildew and starts to smell.

Back home

  • Dry the tent, tarp and sleeping bags
  • Clean and dry the cooler, lid open
  • Restock the kitchen bin and first aid kit
  • Note what you missed for next time

Fun

Rainy-day ideas and camp games

Rain does not have to end the trip. Put on rain jackets, or stay under the tarp, and try a few of these.

  • Card games and a pocket board game
  • A rain walk to look for mushrooms and frogs
  • Visit the park's visitor center or museum
  • Story circle: each person adds one sentence
  • Draw the view in a nature journal
  • Twenty questions about animals
  • Hot chocolate and a book in the tent
  • Learn three knots with a piece of rope
  • After dark: count stars when the sky clears
  • A short drive to a nearby town or viewpoint
