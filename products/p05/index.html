Trail Ready

An 8-week beginner hiking training plan. Walks, stairs and simple bodyweight moves, no gym needed, with a printable log to track every session.

28 pages8 weeksmi + km, lb + kg
Start here

Who this plan is for

You have a bigger hike coming up: a national park trip, your first 10-miler, a summit with a view. You want to arrive strong, enjoy it, and still feel good the next day. You do not need a gym.

This plan is a good fit if you

  • Can walk for about 20 to 30 minutes at an easy pace today
  • Are new to hiking, or coming back after a long break
  • Have 8 weeks before your big hike (or want a solid base)
  • Can train 5 days a week, mostly 20 to 45 minutes on weekdays
  • Prefer simple sessions you can do at home or near home

What you need

  • Shoes you will hike in: trail runners or hiking shoes, broken in during this plan
  • A daypack with a hip belt if you have one
  • Water bottles: they double as pack weight
  • Stairs or a hill: a stairwell, bleachers, a parking garage or a local hill
  • A sturdy chair and a step or the bottom stair
  • A watch or phone to time sessions

What you will build in 8 weeks

Legs that handle long climbs, knees and ankles used to the way down, a back and hips used to carrying a pack, and the confidence of knowing your shoes, socks, food and pace before the big day. Step by step, with an easier week in the middle.

How to use this plan

Read pages 3 to 7 once. Then print one week page at a time (pages 8 to 15) and stick it on the fridge. Write every session in the log on pages 20 to 23. If life gets in the way, repeat a week rather than skipping ahead.

Before you start

A quick self-check

Most healthy adults can start a walking and bodyweight plan like this one. Answer these questions honestly first.

Has a doctor ever told you that you have a heart condition or high blood pressure?YesNo
Do you get chest pain or pressure at rest, during daily activity or during exercise?YesNo
In the past year, have you lost your balance from dizziness or fainted?YesNo
Do you have a bone, joint or back problem that gets worse with activity?YesNo
Do you have a long-term health condition, or take medicine for one?YesNo
Are you pregnant, or did you give birth in the last few months?YesNo
Are you recovering from an injury, illness or surgery?YesNo

If you answered yes to any question

Talk to your doctor before you start, and take this plan with you. Ask which parts are right for you. A longer free screening form, the PAR-Q+, is at eparmedx.com.

Listen to your body

Training should feel like effort, not pain. Slow down if you cannot speak in short sentences. Feeling tired and a bit sore the next day is normal. Sharp pain, joint pain or pain that makes you limp is not: page 19 explains the difference.

Stop right away and call 911 (US and Canada) if you have chest pain or pressure, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or a racing or irregular heartbeat that does not settle with rest.

How it works

Five short sessions a week

Every week has three walking or hiking days, two strength days and two rest days. Sessions grow a little each week, and week 4 is easier on purpose.

MonStrength
20 to 30 min
TueBrisk walk
WedStrength
20 to 30 min
ThuStairs and hills
FriRest
SatLong hike with pack
SunRest

Move the days to fit your week. Two rules: keep a rest day before the long hike, and do not do two long or hard days in a row.

How hard? Use the talk test

EasyYou can chat in full sentences. Warm-ups, cool-downs, easier weeks.
BriskYou can talk, but not sing. Most walks and long hikes.
HardOnly a few words at a time. Short stair and hill climbs only.

Make it easier

  • Shorten the session by 10 minutes
  • Use the easier strength versions
  • Carry less pack weight
  • Repeat the week before moving on

Make it harder

  • Pick hillier routes
  • Use the harder strength versions
  • Add one round of strength moves
  • Add 2 to 3 lb (1 kg) to the pack, not more

This plan follows the spirit of the CDC guidelines for adults: at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days.

Trail Ready4
Every session

Warm-up and cool-down

Five to eight minutes before every session and five to ten after. Move gently: no bouncing, no forcing a stretch.

Warm-up

2 to 3 min

March or easy walk

March on the spot or walk slowly. Swing your arms. Let your breathing pick up a little.

10 each way

Ankle circles

Lift one foot and draw slow circles with your toes. Both directions, both feet.

10 each leg

Leg swings

Hold a wall or tree. Swing one leg forward and back, small at first, then a bit bigger.

10 each way

Hip circles

Hands on hips, feet apart. Draw big slow circles with your hips.

Cool-down: walk slowly 3 to 5 minutes, then hold each stretch 20 to 30 seconds per side

Calf

Wall calf stretch

Hands on a wall, one foot back, heel down, back leg straight. Lean in until the calf stretches.

Front thigh

Standing quad stretch

Hold a chair. Bend one knee and hold your ankle behind you. Knees together, stand tall.

Back thigh

Hamstring stretch

One heel forward, leg straight, toes up. Bend the other knee and hinge at the hips, back flat.

Front of hip

Kneeling hip stretch

Kneel on a soft surface, one foot forward. Tuck your hips and shift forward gently.

Short on time?

Keep the warm-up and cut the main session shorter instead. Warm muscles and joints handle stairs, hills and strength moves better than cold ones.

Trail Ready5
Strength days

Strength moves for hikers, part 1

Do the six moves on pages 6 and 7 in a row, then rest one to two minutes: that is one round. Your week page says how many rounds. Move slowly and with control.

8 to 12 each leg

Step-ups

Use the bottom stair or a sturdy step about 6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm) high. Place your whole foot on the step, press through the heel and stand up tall. Step down slowly with the other foot. Finish one leg, then switch.

EasierA lower step. Hold a rail or wall for balance.
HarderA higher step, up to about 12 to 16 in (30 to 40 cm), or wear your loaded pack.
8 to 12

Squat to chair

Stand in front of a sturdy chair, feet hip-width apart. Push your hips back and bend your knees until you lightly touch the seat, then stand up. Keep your chest up and your knees in line with your toes.

EasierAdd a cushion to raise the seat, and use your hands to help.
HarderLower for a slow count of three, or hover just above the seat for two seconds.
8 to 10 each leg

Reverse lunge

Stand tall. Step one foot back and bend both knees until the back knee is a few inches above the floor. Push through the front heel to return. The front knee stays over the ankle.

EasierHold a chair or wall, and only bend part way.
HarderWear your loaded pack, or pause for a second at the bottom.

Trail Ready6
Strength days

Strength moves for hikers, part 2

12 to 15

Calf raises

Hold a chair or counter. Rise up onto the balls of your feet as high as you can, pause, then lower slowly. Strong calves help on every climb and keep ankles steady.

EasierSmaller lifts, both hands on the chair.
HarderOne leg at a time, or stand on a stair edge and lower the heels just below the step.
10 to 12

Glute bridge

Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Squeeze your seat and lift your hips until your shoulders, hips and knees make a straight line. Hold two seconds and lower slowly.

EasierA smaller lift. Rest between reps.
HarderHold five seconds at the top, or lift with one leg while the other is straight.
15 to 45 sec

Forearm plank

Forearms on the floor, elbows under shoulders, legs straight. Keep a straight line from head to heels and breathe normally. A strong middle helps you carry a pack and keep your balance.

EasierKnees down, or hands on a kitchen counter.
HarderHold longer, or lift one foot for a few seconds at a time.

Form beats speed

Slow, controlled reps build more strength than fast ones. If a move hurts a joint, switch to the easier version or skip it for that day and tell your doctor or a physical therapist if it keeps happening.

Trail Ready16
Technique

Going down, kindly on your knees

Many hikers find the way down harder on the body than the way up. Gravity speeds you up and your legs work hard to brake. Good technique takes much of the strain away.

1

Short steps

Take small steps, especially on steep or loose ground. Long steps make each landing harder.

2

Soft knees

Keep a slight bend in your knees as you land. Never lock them straight on the way down.

3

Weight over your feet

Stay upright or lean very slightly forward. Leaning back is how feet slide out from under you.

4

Look ahead

Scan two or three steps in front, so you can pick flat spots and avoid loose rock and wet roots.

5

Use poles

Plant them a little ahead of you. Many hikers lengthen them by a few inches (5 to 10 cm) for descents.

6

Slow down

Control beats speed. Take short breaks on long descents to rest your legs and look around.

Stop toe bang

If your toes hit the front of your shoes, re-tie your laces snug across the top of the foot before the descent. A heel-lock lacing loop at the top eyelets holds your heel back. Keep toenails trimmed short.

Stay on the trail

Follow switchbacks all the way. Cutting the corners speeds up erosion, damages plants and is often loose and slippery.

Practice in training

Walk down the stairs slowly on your stair days, and choose long hikes with real descents from week 3. Your legs adapt to downhill work the same way they adapt to climbing.

Trail Ready17
Fuel

Food and water on training hikes

You do not need a special diet to train for a hike. You need enough water, enough food, and to know what your stomach likes before the big day.

Water

Start the hike well hydrated, then sip regularly instead of drinking a lot at once. How much you need depends on heat, effort, climb and your body.

A common starting point is about half a liter (17 oz) per hour of moderate hiking in mild weather, and more in heat or on steep climbs. Use your training hikes to learn your own number.

Water is heavy: one liter weighs about 2.2 lb (1 kg). That is why it counts as pack weight.

Signs you need more: strong thirst, dark urine, a headache or feeling dizzy. On long hot hikes, salty snacks or an electrolyte drink help replace the salt you sweat out.

Food

Eat a normal meal one to three hours before a long hike. On hikes longer than about an hour and a half, have a small snack every hour or so, before you feel empty.

Easy trail snacks

  • Trail mix, nuts or seeds
  • Peanut butter or cheese sandwich
  • Granola or energy bars
  • Dried fruit, bananas, apples
  • Crackers, pretzels, jerky

Carry one more snack than you think you need, and pack out every wrapper.

Nothing new on the big day

Use weeks 5 to 8 to test the exact breakfast, snacks and drinks you plan to bring. A new energy gel or a big unfamiliar meal on the morning of the big hike is a gamble you do not need to take.

Trail Ready18
Listen to your body

Sore or hurt?

Some muscle soreness is a normal part of getting fitter. Pain that signals an injury is different, and it needs rest, not more training.

Normal training soreness

What it feels like
  • A dull ache or stiffness in the muscles
  • Starts later that day or the next day
  • Is worst for a day or two, then fades
  • Feels better once you warm up and move
What to do

Keep moving gently: an easy walk and the cool-down stretches. If you are still very sore on a strength or hill day, do the easier version or swap it for an easy walk.

Warning signs

What it feels like
  • Sharp or stabbing pain
  • Pain in a joint (knee, ankle, hip) rather than a muscle
  • Swelling or bruising
  • Pain that makes you limp or change how you walk
  • Pain that gets worse as you keep going
  • Numbness or tingling
What to do

Stop that activity and rest it. If it is not clearly better within a few days, or it wakes you at night, see a doctor or physical therapist before you train on it.

Blisters start as hot spots

The moment you feel rubbing or heat on your foot, stop and cover it with tape or a blister dressing. Two minutes now saves a painful hike later.

Missed a few days?

It happens. Pick up where you left off. If you missed a week or more, or were ill, repeat the last week you completed before moving on.

Emergency: call 911 (US and Canada)

Chest pain or pressure, severe shortness of breath, fainting, confusion, or a fall with a head injury or a bone that may be broken.

Big hike

Pre-trip checklist

The week before

  • Check trail conditions and alerts with the park or land manager
  • Permits or reservations booked, printed or saved
  • Check the weather forecast, then check it again the day before
  • Look up sunrise and sunset; set a turnaround time
  • Download an offline map of the trail
  • Fill in the trip plan (page 26) and give it to someone
  • Charge phone, headlamp and power bank
  • Trim toenails, check shoes and laces

The night before

  • Pack using this list and the dress rehearsal notes
  • Fill water bottles or bladder
  • Set out clothes and breakfast
  • Sleep early

The Ten Essentials (NPS)

  • Navigation: map, compass or GPS, offline map
  • Headlamp and spare batteries
  • Sun protection: hat, sunglasses, sunscreen
  • First aid kit, including blister care
  • Knife or multi-tool
  • Fire: matches or a lighter
  • Emergency shelter: a space blanket or bivy
  • Extra food
  • Extra water, or a way to treat it
  • Extra clothes: warm layer and rain jacket

Also

  • Broken-in shoes and spare socks
  • Trekking poles
  • Phone, ID, car keys, park pass
  • Whistle
  • Trash bag to pack out everything
