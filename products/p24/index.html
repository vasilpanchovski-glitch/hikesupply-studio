How to use this planner

Good trail food is simple: enough energy to enjoy the whole hike, nothing extra to carry, and nothing left behind. This planner walks you from the idea to the packed bag.

1

Read the short guide

Pages 3 to 9 explain how much food to plan, when to eat, what travels well, and whether you need a stove. Ten minutes of reading.

2

Fill in your plan

Use the day hike planner (page 6), the overnight planner (page 10) or the 3 to 5 day planner (pages 11 and 12). Write in pencil so you can change it.

3

Weigh, shop, repack

Weigh your food (page 13), make the shopping list (page 14), then repack and label everything by day (page 15).

4

Learn from each trip

After the hike, note what you ate, what came home uneaten and what to change (page 19). Your next plan gets better every time.

Printing

Print at Actual size on US Letter. An A4 file and a low-ink printer-friendly file are included. Print the fill-in pages again for every trip; they are yours to reuse for personal use.

The short guide

Why food planning matters

Food is fuel, weight and trash all at once. A few minutes of planning helps with all three.

Energy

Steady legs, clear head

Hiking burns a lot of energy, and the body runs low faster on a climb, in the cold or with a heavy pack. Eating small amounts often keeps your pace steady and your decisions good late in the day, when most slips and wrong turns happen.

Weight

Every extra ounce is carried

Food is often the heaviest thing in a backpack after water. Bringing too much is one of the most common beginner mistakes. Planning by weight per day keeps the pack lighter without leaving you hungry.

Waste

Pack it in, pack it out

Every wrapper, peel and crumb goes home with you. Repacking food at home means less packaging on the trail and less trash to carry out.

Wildlife

Keep animals wild

Bears, raccoons, squirrels and mice learn fast where food is. Careful storage protects your food and protects the animals, which can get into serious trouble once they learn to look for human food.

The three classic mistakes

1

Too much

A heavy bag of food you carry back home. Plan by meals and by weight, and bring one sensible extra, not five.

2

Too little

Running out of energy far from the car. Always carry extra food beyond the plan: it is one of the Ten Essentials.

3

Untested food

A new meal you have never tried, a bar that melts, or crackers that turn to dust. Try new food at home or on a short hike first.

4

Bonus: no water plan

Food and water go together. Salty snacks make you thirsty and dry meals need water to cook. Know where you will refill.

The short guide

How much food should I bring?

There is no single right number. These are common planning ranges that many hikers start from, then adjust after a few trips.

200–300
calories per hour of hiking

A common starting point for day hikes: a small snack every hour or so instead of one big meal.

1.5–2.5 lb
(0.7–1.1 kg) food per person per day

A common planning range for overnight and multi-day trips. Many beginners land near the middle.

100–125
calories per ounce (3.5–4.4 per gram)

A handy target for trail food. Nuts, nut butters and oils are higher; fresh fruit is much lower.

What changes how much you need

  • Body size: bigger bodies need more
  • Distance, pace and the amount of climbing
  • Pack weight: a heavy pack costs energy
  • Cold weather: staying warm burns energy
  • Number of days: appetite often grows after day two
  • Your own appetite and experience

How to find your own number

  1. Start in the middle of the range for your trip type.
  2. On a short trip, write down what you actually ate (page 19).
  3. Weigh what came home uneaten and cut that next time. Hungry in the evening? Add a little more.
  4. Always carry a small extra: one spare meal or a few extra snacks in case the hike runs long.
Not diet or medical advice. These ranges are general planning numbers, not a prescription. If you have diabetes, a heart condition, food allergies, are pregnant, or follow a medical diet, ask your doctor or a registered dietitian how to plan food for long days outdoors.

Day hike snack rhythm

Eat a little, often. Small snacks every hour or so keep energy steady, and short stops are good for checking the map, the weather and your feet.

A sample 5-hour day hike

BeforeA real breakfast with some protein and fat, plus water.
1 hSmall snack: a handful of trail mix.
2 hSalty snack and a good drink of water.
2.5 hLunch at the top or the lake.
3.5 hSomething sweet for the way down.
AfterA snack in the car, water, rest.

Good habits

  • Eat before you feel hungry or tired
  • Mix salty and sweet: salt helps replace what you sweat out
  • Drink with every snack stop
  • Keep today's snacks in a hip belt or jacket pocket so stops stay short
  • Eat more on long climbs and in the cold
  • Keep one backup snack you do not touch unless the day runs long

What travels well

Hot daysCold days
Salted nuts, pretzels, crackersNut butter packets, trail mix
Dried fruit, fig barsChocolate and cookies
Hard cheese (eat early)Cheese and salami-style sausage
Fresh fruit on day oneWarm drink in an insulated bottle
Electrolyte drink mixSoft bars kept in an inside pocket

Chocolate and coated candy melt in the heat; many bars turn rock hard in the cold.

Feeling weak, shaky, grumpy or confused on the trail? Stop, sit, eat something and drink. If you or a partner does not get better quickly, or feels very unwell, turn back and get help.

Trail Food Planner6
Ideas

No-cook trail food ideas

No stove, no fuel, no washing up. Mix from each group so every snack stop has something salty, something sweet and something filling.

Salty

Crunch and salt

  • Salted nuts and seeds
  • Pretzels, crackers, rice crackers
  • Roasted chickpeas or broad beans
  • Sturdy chips in a hard container
Sweet

Quick energy

  • Dried fruit: apricots, mango, dates, raisins
  • Fig bars and soft cookies
  • Gummies and hard candy
  • Chocolate on cool days
Filling

Protein and fat

  • Jerky or meat sticks
  • Tuna or chicken in a pouch
  • Hard cheese, eaten in the first day or two
  • Peanut, almond or sunflower butter packets
Base

Bread and grains

  • Tortillas: flat, sturdy, last for days
  • Bagels and pita
  • Granola or muesli
  • Bars you have tried at home
Day one only

Fresh treats

  • Apples, oranges, baby carrots
  • A packed sandwich
  • Anything that needs a cooler, eaten early
Drinks

In the bottle

  • Electrolyte powder
  • Instant coffee or tea (works cold too)
  • Powdered milk for cereal

Easy no-cook combos

Trail wrap: tortilla, peanut butter, honey and banana chips.

Cracker lunch: crackers, hard cheese, salami-style sausage, dried apricots.

Tuna wrap: tortilla, tuna pouch, mayo or olive oil packet, salt and pepper.

Cold cereal: granola and powdered milk, add cold water at the stop.

Trail Food Planner7
Ideas

Boil-only meal ideas

Boil water, pour, stir, cover and wait. These simple combos use food from a normal grocery store. Follow the cooking time on each package.

Breakfast

Warm and sweet

Loaded oatmeal

Instant oats, powdered milk, brown sugar, raisins and chopped walnuts. Bag it at home, add hot water in camp.

Savory

Cheesy grits

Instant grits, a little hard cheese and a pinch of salt and pepper. Add a crumbled meat stick if you like.

Fast

Granola with hot milk

Granola with powdered milk and hot water. Good on a cold morning when you want to move quickly.

Drink

Coffee or cocoa

Instant coffee, tea bags or hot cocoa mix. A warm drink makes a cold start much easier.

Dinner

Quick

Couscous with tuna

Couscous, an olive oil packet, a tuna or chicken pouch and dried herbs. Couscous only needs to sit in hot water.

Comfort

Mashed potatoes

Instant mashed potatoes, hard cheese, shelf-stable bacon bits and black pepper.

Salty

Peanut noodles

Instant ramen, a peanut butter packet, a soy sauce packet and dried vegetables. Use only part of the flavor packet.

Filling

Rice and beans

Instant rice, dehydrated refried beans and taco seasoning, rolled in a tortilla.

Ready-made meals

Freeze-dried meals from an outdoor store are light and easy. Use the amount of water and the soaking time on the bag, and try one at home first.

Save fuel

Put the lid on the pot, turn the stove off once the water boils, and keep the soaking meal warm inside a jacket or an insulated pouch.

Trail Food Planner8
The short guide

Stove or no stove?

For a day hike, most people need no stove at all. For overnights, choose the simplest option that fits your trip and the local fire rules.

No stove

Cold food and cold soak

Lightest and simplest. No fuel and no fire risk. Some hikers soak couscous, oats or instant noodles in cold water for an hour.

Best for: warm weather, short trips, fire bans.

Canister stove

Screw-on gas

Fast, easy and quiet, with a valve you can turn off. Usually the easiest choice for a beginner. Performance can drop when it is close to or below freezing.

Best for: most overnight trips.

Alcohol stove

Small liquid-fuel cup

Very light and simple, but slower, hard to see the flame, and often not allowed during fire restrictions because it cannot be turned off.

Best for: experienced hikers, mild weather.

Fuel planning basics

  1. At home, weigh your fuel canister, boil the amount of water you use for one meal, then weigh it again. The difference is your fuel per boil.
  2. Count your boils: meals plus hot drinks for the whole trip.
  3. Fuel per boil times boils is what you need. Bring a little extra: wind, cold and high elevation all use more.
  4. Write your numbers here:

Stove safety. Never cook inside a tent or a closed vestibule: there is a risk of fire and of carbon monoxide poisoning. Read and follow your stove maker's instructions, including what they say about windscreens. Check fire restrictions for your area before every trip: during fire bans some places allow only stoves with an on/off valve, and some allow no flame at all.

Trail Food Planner14
Checklist

Repack and label checklist

Repacking at home makes food lighter, smaller, easier to find, and leaves less trash on the trail.

Before you pack

  • Take food out of boxes and big packaging
  • Cut out the cooking directions and put them in the bag
  • Bag each meal: one bag, one meal
  • Squeeze the air out of every bag
  • Double-bag powders and anything crumbly
  • Put oils and sauces in small leak-proof bottles
  • Label each bag with the day, the meal and the water to add
  • Weigh it all and fill in page 13

In the pack

  • One bag per day, in the order you will eat it
  • Today's snacks in a pocket you can reach
  • Day one lunch on top
  • Spare food packed and marked as spare
  • An empty bag just for trash and wrappers
  • Food fits in the canister or bag you will store it in
  • Lighter or matches packed with the stove
  • A copy of this menu left at home with your trip plan

Pack out every wrapper

Orange peels, apple cores and nut shells are trash too: they take a long time to break down and teach animals to look for food. Everything that came in goes back out.

Trail Food Planner15
The short guide

Food storage: bears and critters

The rule is set by the place, not by you. Before every overnight trip, check the park or forest website or call the ranger station for its food storage rule.

Hard-sided

Bear canister

A rigid, locking container. Required in some places, for example much of Yosemite's wilderness and parts of New York's Adirondacks. Use an approved model where one is required.

Provided

Food lockers and poles

Many campgrounds and some backcountry sites have metal food lockers or bear poles. Use them for all food and scented items, and close them every time.

Where allowed

Hanging food

A classic method, often taught as at least 10 ft (3 m) up and 4 ft (1.2 m) from the trunk. It is hard to do well, and many areas no longer accept it.

Store everything with a smell

  • All food, snacks and drink mixes
  • Trash and wrappers
  • Toothpaste, sunscreen, lip balm, wipes
  • Cooking pot and stove

Good camp habits

  • Never keep food in the tent
  • Cook and store food well away from where you sleep; 200 ft (about 60 m) is a common guideline
  • Do not leave your pack open or alone
  • Never feed wildlife, not even squirrels or birds
In bear country, learn the local advice before you go: park and forest websites explain what to do if you meet a bear in that area. Rules and advice differ between black bear and grizzly country.

Trail Food Planner17
The short guide

Food in hot and cold weather

The same snack can melt into a puddle in July and freeze into a brick in January. Plan for the weather you expect.

Hot days

Keep it from melting or spoiling

  • Skip chocolate and coated candy; pick dried fruit, gummies and hard candy
  • Choose hard cheese over soft cheese and eat it early
  • Eat fresh and perishable food on day one
  • Keep food deep in the pack, away from the sun
  • Salty snacks plus electrolyte drink mix help replace what you sweat out
  • Drink often: start the hike well hydrated
Cold days

Keep it soft and keep eating

  • Carry today's snacks in an inside pocket so they stay soft
  • Eat more often: staying warm uses energy
  • A warm drink in an insulated bottle helps at every stop
  • Keep water bottles in an insulated sleeve or inside the pack
  • Storing a bottle upside down helps stop ice forming at the lid
  • Canister stoves can weaken in freezing weather; keep the canister warm before use

Water and food work together

Dry trail food needs water: to drink with salty snacks and to cook boil-only meals. Plan where you will refill, carry a way to treat water from streams and lakes, and follow the instructions of your filter or treatment. You can still get dehydrated in cold weather, so keep drinking even when you do not feel thirsty.
