Two of you on the trail

A dog makes a hike better: more excited at the trailhead, more curious on the way, happier at the top. This guide helps you plan hikes that are safe and fun for both of you, and polite to everyone else.

This guide is for you if

  • Your dog walks well around the neighborhood and you want to go further
  • You are new to hiking, new to dogs, or both
  • You hike day trips in the US or Canada
  • You want simple pack lists and a hike log

What is inside

  • Pages 3 to 12: the guide, one topic per page
  • Pages 13 to 15: first aid kit and pack lists
  • Pages 16 and 17: dog ID card and trail plan
  • Pages 18 and 19: a log for 16 hikes

Talk to your vet first

Before your dog's first longer hikes, ask your vet whether your dog is ready and how far to go. This matters most for puppies (their joints are still growing), older dogs, dogs with health problems or extra weight, and flat-faced breeds such as pugs and bulldogs, which overheat easily. Ask about tick and flea prevention and vaccines for where you hike, too.

The three habits that matter most

Leash

On the leash, close to you, every time the rules ask for it.

Water

Clean water for your dog at every stop, carried from home.

Bags

Every poop bagged and carried out, all the way to the bin.

How to use it

Read the guide once. Print the checklists, ID card and log, and keep a filled-in card in your pack. Units are in miles and km, °F and °C.

General information, not veterinary advice. Dog rules change: always check the official page for the place you hike.

Work on manners

Practice "sit", "wait" and "leave it" on busy paths in town. A short training class is worth it if your dog pulls hard or reacts to other dogs. Reward calm behavior with a treat.

Work on fitness

Add a little distance to your regular walks each week, with some hills. Fitness builds over weeks, not in one big weekend. Page 4 has a simple plan.

Build up slowly: a 4-week start

This is a gentle example for a healthy adult dog that already walks every day. Your vet may suggest something different, and that advice comes first.

12 mi / 3 km
mostly flat		Harness and leash on the trail, water breaks, a short sit at a viewpoint. Watch how your dog handles new smells and sounds.

Keep the other walks in the week short and easy. If your dog is stiff, limping or very tired the next day, go back a step and talk to your vet if it does not clear up.

Signs your dog needs a rest

  • Lagging behind you or lying down on the trail
  • Heavy panting that does not settle after a few minutes in the shade
  • Looking for every bit of shade or water
  • Licking or favoring a paw

Signs to stop and turn back

  • Limping, or refusing to keep going
  • Stumbling, confusion or vomiting
  • Signs of overheating (page 8)
  • A cut pad or a paw that bleeds

On every hike day

Short warm-up walk at the trailhead, water at every stop, a paw check halfway, and a slow last mile to cool down before the car.

The dog sets the pace and the distance, not the plan. Turning back early is always a good hike.

Hiking With Your Dog4
Rules

Where dogs can hike

This is the most common surprise for new dog hikers: the place matters. Many beautiful parks keep dogs off their trails. Always check before you drive there.

US national parksThe strictest. In most parks, pets are allowed in campgrounds, parking areas and along roads, but not on most trails. A few parks welcome leashed dogs on many trails. Rules differ park by park, so read the "Pets" page on the park's own nps.gov site.
US national forests and BLM landUsually the most dog-friendly. Dogs are often allowed on trails, with a leash required in campgrounds and other developed areas. Some areas have extra rules. Ask the local ranger district.
State parksMany allow leashed dogs on trails. Leash length limits are common (often 6 ft / 1.8 m). Some parks or trails do not allow dogs. Check the park's page.
CanadaIn Parks Canada national parks, dogs must be on a leash at all times, and some trails and areas close to dogs, often because of wildlife. Provincial parks set their own rules. Check the park's official page.
Local and city trailsRules vary. Look for signs at the trailhead and respect off-leash areas only where they are clearly marked.

The BARK Ranger rules

Pet manners in four letters, from the National Park Service:

B

Bag your pet's waste and carry it out.

A

Always leash your pet.

R

Respect wildlife.

K

Know where you can go.

Check the rules in two minutes

Search the park name plus "pets", open the official page (.gov, .gc.ca or the state or provincial park site) and find the trails open to dogs. Not clear? Call the visitor center.

Hiking With Your Dog5
On the trail

Trail manners

Not everyone loves dogs, and some people are afraid of them. A calm, close, leashed dog keeps trails open to dogs for everyone.

1

Keep the leash on

Even a friendly dog with great recall can chase a deer or run up to a scared child. Follow the leash rules, and use a fixed leash, not a long retractable one, on narrow trails.

2

Step aside and wait

When you meet other hikers, move to the side of the trail, ask your dog to sit, and let them pass. Hold the leash short until they are by.

3

Ask before saying hello

Do not let your dog greet other dogs or people unless the other person says yes. "Is it OK if they say hi?" is all it takes.

4

Keep the noise down

Barking carries far in quiet places. If your dog barks at everything, practice on shorter, quieter trails first.

Meeting horses

Horses can be startled by dogs. Step off the trail, ideally on the downhill side, keep your dog close and sitting, and say hello to the rider so the horse knows you are a person. Ask the rider what they would like you to do.

Meeting bikes: move aside with your dog on the far side from the bike, on a short leash.

Off-leash areas

Let your dog off leash only where the rules clearly allow it, and only if it comes back the first time you call, even near wildlife and other dogs.

People who are scared of dogs. If someone looks nervous, steps back or picks up a child, give them extra room, keep your dog close and do not say "he is friendly". Just let them pass.
Hiking With Your Dog6
Fuel

Water and food on the trail

Your dog cannot tell you it is thirsty, and it cannot carry a drink bottle in its paw. Plan water and snacks for two.

Water

  • Carry water for your dog as well as for yourself. Dogs often need more on a hike than at home, especially when it is warm
  • Offer water at every stop, about every 15 to 30 minutes in warm weather, even if your dog did not ask
  • Use a collapsible bowl or a dog bottle with a built-in trough
  • Plan to finish the hike with some water left over
  • Ask your vet how much water your dog needs for the hikes you plan

Food

  • Bring dog snacks or a small portion of their usual food
  • Offer a small snack at longer breaks, rather than one big meal
  • Many vets advise against a big meal just before or right after hard exercise, especially for large, deep-chested breeds. Ask yours about timing
  • Pack out every wrapper and dropped crumb

After the hike

Offer water first. Give the main meal later, once your dog has cooled down and settled. A tired dog that will not eat or drink after a hike should see a vet.

Never share these on the trail

Some human hiking snacks are poisonous to dogs, including chocolate, grapes and raisins (often in trail mix), and anything with xylitol (some gums, candies and peanut butters). Keep your snacks zipped and out of reach.

Collapsible bowl, dog water bottle and snacks are on the dog pack list on page 14.

Hiking With Your Dog7
Weather

Heat and cold

Dogs cool down mainly by panting, which works much less well than sweating. Heat is one of the biggest risks for hiking dogs.

On warm days

  • Hike early in the morning or in the evening
  • Pick shady trails, ideally with safe water nearby
  • Check the ground: if you cannot keep the back of your hand on pavement or rock for 7 seconds, it is too hot for paws
  • Rest in the shade often and offer water
  • Shorten the hike, or leave your dog home on hot days

On cold days

  • Short-coated, small and older dogs may need a dog jacket
  • Snow can pack into balls between the toes: check paws often
  • Booties protect paws from ice and road salt; practice with them at home first
  • Dry your dog off before a long car ride home
  • Watch for shivering, lifting paws or slowing down

Signs of overheating

  • Very heavy, fast panting
  • Lots of drool, or very red gums and tongue
  • Weakness, stumbling or confusion
  • Vomiting or diarrhea
  • Collapse

What to do right away

  • Stop and move into the shade
  • Offer small amounts of water
  • Wet your dog with cool (not ice-cold) water, especially the belly and paws, and fan it
  • Call a vet and head for help: overheating can be an emergency even if your dog seems to recover
Never leave your dog in a parked car on a warm day, not even for a few minutes with the windows cracked. Cars heat up fast, even in the shade.

Flat-faced breeds, thick-coated breeds, overweight, older and very young dogs overheat more easily. Ask your vet what temperatures are safe for yours.

Hiking With Your Dog8
After every hike

Paws and ticks

Two quick checks turn small problems into nothing. Do them at the car, before your dog jumps in.

Paw check

  • Look at each pad for cuts, cracks, blisters or worn, shiny patches
  • Spread the toes and remove burrs, grass seeds, small stones and snow
  • Feel for heat or swelling, and watch for limping or licking later that day
  • Keep nails trimmed: long nails catch and split on rocks
  • Brush the coat: it finds burrs, seeds and ticks hiding in the fur
  • A small cut: rinse with clean water and keep it clean. A deep or bleeding cut: cover it, keep your dog off it and see a vet

Tick check, for the dog

Run your hands slowly over your dog and feel for small bumps. Look closely at the places ticks like most:

Head and ears
inside and around the ears

Neck
under the collar or harness

Legs
armpits, between the toes

Belly and tail
groin, under the tail

If you find one, remove it with fine-tipped tweezers or a tick tool: grip it close to the skin and pull straight out with steady pressure. Clean the spot. Ask your vet about tick prevention for where you hike.

Then watch the spot over the next few weeks. If your dog seems unwell, tired or stiff, or stops eating, call your vet and mention the tick bite.

And for you

Check yourself too: behind the knees, the waist, the hairline and behind the ears. A shower soon after the hike helps. A dog can also carry ticks into the car and home.

Hiking With Your Dog9
Wild neighbors

Wildlife

A leash is the best wildlife protection there is. A dog that chases an animal can get hurt, lost, or bring trouble back to you.

Bears

Keep your dog close

An off-leash dog can surprise a bear and run back to you with the bear behind it. Keep your dog leashed, make noise on blind corners, and store dog food like your own.

Snakes

Stay on the trail

Keep your dog on the path, away from tall grass, rock piles and logs. If you think it was bitten, keep it calm, carry it if you can and get to a vet right away.

Porcupines

Leave quills to a vet

Curious dogs often get a face full of quills. Do not pull them yourself: they break, and a dog in pain may bite. Go to a vet.

Others to know about

Deer, elk, mooseBig animals can kick hard, especially mothers with young. Give them lots of room and turn around if one is on the trail.
CoyotesUsually shy, but they may follow or test a dog. Keep your dog leashed and close, and leave calmly.
SkunksMostly out at dawn and dusk. A sprayed dog is unpleasant but usually fine; rinse its eyes with clean water.
Small animalsSquirrels and marmots are wildlife too. Do not let your dog chase or dig after them.

Hunting season

In many forests, hunting is allowed in fall. Put a bright orange vest or bandana on your dog and wear something bright yourself. Check the hunting dates on your state or provincial wildlife agency's site.

Learn which animals live where you hike from the park or forest's official page, and follow its advice.

Hiking With Your Dog10
Water

Streams, lakes and crossings

Water is the best part of the hike for many dogs. A few simple habits keep it that way.

Carry clean water instead

Streams and lakes can carry germs and parasites that make dogs sick, the same ones that make people sick. The easy answer: bring clean water from home and offer it often, so your dog is less tempted to drink from puddles and ponds.

Stay away from scummy water. Toxic algae (blue-green algae) can make water look green, blue-green or like spilled paint, and it can be deadly for dogs. If water looks or smells odd, or there is a warning sign, keep your dog out of it and do not let it drink or lick its fur. If your dog has been in it, rinse it with clean water and call a vet.

Crossing streams

  • Cross only where the water is slow and shallow, well below your dog's chest
  • If the water is fast or deep, do not cross. Turn back or find another way
  • Keep your dog on leash, or carry a small dog across
  • A dog life jacket with a handle helps for swimming in calm lakes
  • Never throw sticks or balls into moving water

Swimming

  • Let your dog go in at its own pace; not every dog is a swimmer
  • Stay out of cold water, strong currents and big waves; rinse off lake water after
  • Watch for fishing hooks and line near the shore

Ask your vet

Ask about vaccines and prevention for diseases your dog can catch from water and wildlife in your area. The answer depends on where you live and hike.

Hiking With Your Dog11
Leave No Trace

Poop and Leave No Trace

Dog waste is not "natural" on a trail. It can pollute water, spread disease to wildlife and other dogs, and it is one of the most common complaints about dogs on trails.

1

Bag it

Pick it up every time, right away, with a bag. Bring more bags than you think you need.

2

Carry it out

Never leave a bag beside the trail "for the way back". Many are forgotten. A small odor-proof bag or a clip-on pouch makes carrying easy.

3

Follow local rules

Some places allow burying dog waste in a cathole away from water and trails; many ask you to pack it out. When in doubt, pack it out.

4

Stay on the trail

Keep your dog on the path, out of fragile plants, meadows and lake shores, and away from other people's campsites.

The seven Leave No Trace principles, with a dog

  • Plan ahead: check the dog rules and the weather for that day
  • Travel on durable surfaces: trails and rock, not meadows
  • Dispose of waste properly: bag it, carry it out
  • Leave what you find: no digging, no chewing up plants
  • Respect wildlife: leash on, no chasing, no feeding
  • Minimize campfire impacts: keep your dog away from fire rings and hot coals
  • Be considerate of other visitors: quiet, close and polite

Learn more about Leave No Trace at lnt.org.

Checklist

Dog first aid kit

A small kit for the scrapes and cuts that happen on a trail, and the phone numbers you need when it is more than that. It does not replace a vet.

Wound care

  • Gauze pads and a gauze roll
  • Non-stick wound pads
  • Self-stick bandage wrap (vet wrap)
  • Medical tape
  • Saline or clean water to rinse wounds
  • Blunt-tip scissors

Tools

  • Tick remover or fine-tipped tweezers
  • Disposable gloves
  • A spare dog bootie or sock to cover a hurt paw
  • A small towel

Just in case

  • Emergency blanket
  • A soft muzzle or a strip of cloth (a dog in pain may bite, even its owner)
  • A spare leash
  • Any medicine your vet prescribed for your dog

Paperwork

  • Your vet's phone number
  • Nearest 24-hour emergency vet clinic near the trail
  • Pet poison helpline number
  • Photo of your dog and vaccine records on your phone
No human painkillers. Many common human medicines, including ibuprofen and naproxen, are dangerous for dogs. Never give your dog any medicine unless a vet tells you to.

Check it twice a year

Replace anything used or out of date, and update the phone numbers when you travel somewhere new.

Consider a pet first aid class: many animal welfare groups and some vets offer one. Write the phone numbers on the ID card on page 16.

Checklist

Your pack for a hike with your dog

Your usual day-hike kit, plus a few things that make hiking with a dog easier.

The Ten Essentials

  • Navigation: map, compass or GPS, and you know how to use them
  • Headlamp with spare batteries
  • Sun protection: sunglasses, sunscreen, hat
  • First aid kit (yours, plus the dog kit)
  • Knife or multi-tool
  • Fire starter
  • Emergency shelter, such as a space blanket
  • Extra food
  • Extra water
  • Extra clothes

Dog extras for you

  • Hands-free waist leash, for easier climbing
  • A second water bottle, just for the dog
  • Gloves for a strong puller
  • Bear spray in bear country, if the land manager recommends it
  • Plastic bag to keep the car seat clean

Before you leave home

  • Check the dog rules for this exact trail
  • Check the weather and temperature
  • Share your trail plan (page 17)
  • Charge your phone

For the drive home

  • Water and a bowl in the car
  • Old towel or sheet for the seat
  • A safe spot: crate, dog seat belt or barrier
  • Snack for after the cool-down

Tip: one shared bag

Keep a "dog hike bag" packed by the door with the bowl, bags, first aid kit, towel and spare leash. Add fresh water and snacks and you are out the door in two minutes.
