How to use this logbook

Three of the best-known peak lists in the US, each with a progress board to color in and a log card for every summit. Work on one list or all three.

1

Read about your list

Each list starts with a page on how it works, access and permits, and how its club recognizes finishers.

2

Color the board

Fill in the circle and write the date when you stand on top. The board shows your whole list at a glance.

3

Fill in the log card

One card per peak: date, route, partners, weather, times and a few notes. The dashed circle is for a sticker, a stamp or your initials.

4

Finish strong

Winter round tracker, your own list, photo pages, trip notes, a gear check and a finisher certificate to fill in.

Print tips

Print at 100% (actual size) on US Letter. A heavier paper (32 lb or card stock) makes the log cards last in a pack. The printer-friendly version uses less ink. Elevations are in feet with meters in gray (1 ft = 0.3048 m).

Before a big peak

Honest safety basics

A summit is optional. Getting home is not. These basics apply to all three lists.

1

Start early

In the Colorado summer, thunderstorms often build by early afternoon. Aim to be off the summit and exposed ridges before they form. If you hear thunder, head down.

2

Set a turnaround time

Decide before you leave when you will turn back, summit or not, and keep to it. Most trouble starts late in the day.

3

Check official forecasts

Use mountain forecasts, not the town forecast. For the Presidential Range, read the Mount Washington Observatory higher summits forecast. Weather above treeline can turn severe fast.

4

Know the route

Carry a map and know how to use it. Phones die in the cold. Some peaks have no marked trail, and some routes need scrambling or real climbing skills.

5

Respect the altitude

Colorado 14ers start above 14,000 ft (4,267 m). Headache, nausea or dizziness can be signs of altitude sickness. Going down is the best fix. Drink water and eat often.

6

Tell someone

Leave your route and return time with a person at home, and tell them who to call if you are late.

Leave No Trace

Stay on the trail or herd path, walk on rock above treeline to protect fragile alpine plants, pack out all trash, and take only photos from the summit.

General information only. Conditions, closures and rules change. Check the land manager (Forest Service, National Park Service, NY DEC, state agencies) before every hike, hike within your ability and turn back when in doubt.

List 1

The Colorado 14ers

The mountains in Colorado that rise above 14,000 feet (4,267 m). Most lists count 58 named peaks. On 14ers.com, 52 of them stand on their own and 6 are named summits that rise less than 300 ft above the saddle to a higher neighbor (marked unranked on the board).

58named peaks on the board
14,438 ftMount Elbert, the highest (4,401 m)
7mountain ranges

From walk-ups to real climbs

Some 14ers have a well-built trail to the top, such as Quandary, Bierstadt or Elbert by their standard routes. Others, like Capitol, Little Bear, Pyramid and the Maroon Bells, need exposed scrambling on loose rock where a fall can be fatal. Read a current route description, and build up from easier peaks.

Check access before you go

Most 14ers are on National Forest or National Park land, but not all access is public. Culebra Peak is on a private ranch that limits visits and charges a fee. Some peaks, trailheads and parking areas cross private land or charge parking or entrance fees, and access can change from one season to the next. Longs Peak is in Rocky Mountain National Park, which has its own entrance and reservation rules. Check the current rules with the land manager or the landowner.

Recognition

There is no single official club for the Colorado list. Many hikers log their climbs on 14ers.com or join the Colorado Mountain Club. This logbook is your own record.

Snow lingers high up into early summer, and summer afternoons bring lightning. Mount Blue Sky was called Mount Evans until 2023, so older guides use the old name.

List 2

The New Hampshire 4,000-Footers

The 48 peaks in New Hampshire's White Mountains that reach 4,000 feet (1,219 m) and rise at least 200 ft above the col that joins them to a higher neighbor. The list is kept by the Four Thousand Footer Committee of the Appalachian Mountain Club.

48peaks on the list
6,288 ftMount Washington, the highest (1,917 m)
1957the year the list began

Small numbers, big days

These peaks are lower than Colorado's, but the trails are often steep, rocky and rooty, and the climbs are big from the valley floor. Some peaks, like Owl's Head and Isolation, are long days far from any road. Above treeline on the Presidential Range, weather can be among the worst in the country, even in summer.

Before you go

Most of these peaks are in the White Mountain National Forest. Many trailheads need a parking pass or charge a fee, and some roads close in winter. Check the Forest Service and the Mount Washington Observatory higher summits forecast before you head up.

Recognition

The Four Thousand Footer Committee recognizes hikers who climb all 48 on foot. Read the rules and the application on the committee's website and send it in when you finish. There are separate lists and rules for winter rounds.

The board shows the elevations exactly as the committee publishes them. A few are estimates from map contours, and some peaks have been resurveyed over the years.

List 3

The Adirondack High Peaks

The 46 High Peaks of New York's Adirondack Mountains, first climbed as a set in the 1920s. The list is kept by the Adirondack Forty-Sixers, and it keeps its original 46, even though later surveys found four of them just under 4,000 ft.

46peaks on the list
5,344 ftMount Marcy, the highest in New York (1,629 m)
About 20peaks with no official marked trail

Trailless peaks

Many of the 46 have no official marked trail, only unmarked herd paths. Expect mud, rock slabs, blowdown and some route finding. Carry a map and compass, and know how to use them before you rely on them.

Rules to check

Most of the High Peaks are on New York State Forest Preserve land managed by the Department of Environmental Conservation (DEC). Parking at the Adirondack Mountain Reserve trailhead near St. Huberts needs a free reservation in the busy season, roadside parking is limited on some highways, group sizes are limited, and overnight campers in the Eastern High Peaks must carry a bear-resistant food canister in season. In spring mud season, DEC asks hikers to stay off high trails. Check DEC before every trip.

Recognition

The Adirondack Forty-Sixers recognize hikers who climb all 46. Follow the club's instructions for reporting your climbs.

Elevations on the board are the traditional heights the list has always used. Blake, Cliff, Nye and Couchsachraga are below 4,000 ft by later surveys and are still part of the 46.

Peak Bagger's Logbook35
Gear

Summit day gear check

Every peak

  • Map, and a compass you can use
  • Phone with offline maps, and a power bank
  • Headlamp with spare batteries
  • Rain jacket and a warm layer
  • Hat and gloves (yes, in summer too)
  • Plenty of water and salty snacks
  • Sunscreen, sunglasses and lip balm
  • First aid kit and blister care
  • Whistle and an emergency blanket
  • Trip plan left with someone at home

High, cold or long days

  • Helmet for loose, rocky routes
  • Traction devices for ice
  • Snowshoes for deep snow
  • Insulated jacket and spare mittens
  • Insulated bottle with a warm drink
  • Goggles or a face cover for wind
  • Extra food for a slower day
  • Water filter or treatment
  • Trekking poles
  • Bear canister where it is required

Sources

Checked against the official lists

Peak names and elevations were checked against the sources below on November 4, 2026. Lists, names and surveys change from time to time, so check the source if a number matters to you.

Colorado 14ers

14ers.com

All 58 named 14ers with the elevations 14ers.com publishes. "Unranked" peaks rise less than 300 ft above the saddle to a higher neighbor.

NH 4,000-Footers

amc4000footer.org

The 48 peaks and elevations of the AMC Four Thousand Footer Committee. Some are estimates from map contours. Finisher applications are on this site.

Adirondack 46

adk46er.org

The Adirondack Forty-Sixers list with its traditional elevations, and how the club recognizes finishers.

Rules and conditions

fs.usda.gov · nps.gov · dec.ny.gov

Trail conditions, closures, parking reservations, permits and food storage rules come from the land managers. Mountain forecasts: weather.gov and mountwashington.org.
