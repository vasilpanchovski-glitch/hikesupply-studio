Start here

From day hiker to first night out

You already enjoy day hikes. Now you want to watch the sun go down from camp, sleep under the trees and wake up on the trail. This guide gets you there with one easy, well-planned night, and a second one if you love it.

This guide is for you if you

  • Are comfortable on day hikes of 5 to 8 mi (8 to 13 km)
  • Have never backpacked, or only went once with someone else leading
  • Want a first trip of 1 or 2 nights, not a long expedition
  • Would rather borrow or rent than buy a full kit on day one

What is inside

  • Pages 3 to 17: the guide, one topic per page
  • Pages 18 to 21: gear checklist, pack weight and packing
  • Pages 22 to 26: meals, trip plan and night-before checks
  • Pages 27 to 31: trip logs, sources and a glossary

The one rule of a first trip

Make it easy. Short distance, simple food, a campsite you know exists, and a way out if the weather turns. A great first night is the one that makes you want a second.

How to use it

Read the guide pages once, about six weeks before you go. Then print the planners (pages 18 to 26) and fill them in as you get ready. Every distance and weight is given in both US and metric units.

No maps or GPS tracks are included on purpose: you will pick your route from official park and forest pages, where conditions are kept up to date. Page 4 shows you how.

Do two or three day hikes with a loaded pack. Pitch the tent in the yard or a park. Car camp for one night with your sleeping bag and pad: it is the best test of a sleep system.

A partner who has backpacked before is the easiest first trip of all. You learn by watching, share the gear weight and have help if something goes wrong.

First Backpacking Trip3
Planning

Pick an easy first route

The route decides most of how your trip will feel. For a first night, choose the boring-sounding option. You can be ambitious on trip three.

1

Short

About 2 to 5 mi (3 to 8 km) to camp. A full pack makes every mile feel longer, and you want daylight left to set up camp and eat.

2

Water nearby

A lake, a stream that flows all year, or a tap at a campground. Check recent trip reports or ask rangers: small streams can dry up in late summer.

3

Established campsites

Designated or well-used sites, ideally with a fire ring, food lockers or a privy. You will not have to judge a site from scratch.

4

A bail-out option

An out-and-back or a loop with a short way back to the car. If the weather turns or a knee hurts, you can be home tonight.

Where to find first-trip routes

State parksMany have hike-in or backpack campsites a short walk from the parking lot. Often bookable online. A great first step.
National forestsLots of trails and dispersed camping, often with fewer rules. Call the ranger district for advice and current conditions.
National parksBeautiful and well signed, but most require a backcountry permit and some fill months ahead. Check the park's own website.
CanadaProvincial parks and Parks Canada sites have backcountry campsites, usually reserved ahead online.
Permits and rules: look up whether you need a permit, a campsite reservation or a bear canister before you commit to a route. Search the park or forest name plus "backcountry permit", use the official site (.gov, recreation.gov or the provincial or Parks Canada site), and follow what it says.

First Backpacking Trip5
Gear

The big three, part 1: pack and shelter

Your pack, your shelter and your sleep system are the heaviest and most expensive things you will carry. For a first trip, they only need to work, fit and keep you dry.

The pack

For 1 or 2 nights, most people use a pack of about 40 to 65 liters. More important than the size is the fit: the hip belt should sit on the top of your hip bones and carry most of the weight, with the shoulder straps snug but not taking the load.

  • Fit it with weight inside: an outdoor store can help you adjust it
  • Walk around with 20 lb (9 kg) in it before you rent or buy
  • A pack liner or a strong trash bag inside keeps your sleeping bag and clothes dry

The shelter

A freestanding two-person tent is the easiest choice for a first trip: it stands up on its own, and two people can share the weight (one carries the body, the other the fly and poles).

  • Practice pitching it at home, and once more in the dark with a headlamp
  • Count the stakes and poles before you leave
  • Bring the rain fly even if the forecast is clear
  • A footprint or groundsheet is optional; a clean, smooth site matters more
  • On the test pitch, check zippers, seams and that the fly does not touch the inner tent

Shared gear saves weight

With a partner, share one tent, one stove, one pot and one water filter. Split them so you both carry about the same.

First Backpacking Trip6
Gear

The big three, part 2: sleep system

Your sleep system is a sleeping bag (or quilt) plus a sleeping pad. They work as a team: the bag keeps warm air around you, and the pad stops the cold ground from pulling heat out from below.

Page 8 explains the ratings. For a first summer trip at low elevation, a bag with a comfort rating around 35 to 40°F (2 to 4°C) and a pad that is at least a three-season pad is a common starting point. Always check the expected low for your campsite and choose for that.

First Backpacking Trip7
Gear

Sleeping warm

Most first-night problems are about being cold. Nights in the mountains can drop far below daytime temperatures, even in summer. Here is how to read the labels and stay warm.

Sleeping bag ratings

Many bags are tested to a standard (EN or ISO) and show two numbers. Comfort is the temperature at which a typical cold sleeper stays comfortable. Limit is where a typical warm sleeper can sleep curled up. Plan with the comfort number, not the limit.

Sleeping pad R-value

R-value measures how well a pad blocks cold from the ground. Higher is warmer, and values from pads tested to the same standard can be added up when you stack two pads. Summer pads are often around 2; three-season pads are higher. Pick for the coldest night you expect.

Layers for the night

  • Change into dry clothes for sleeping: damp hiking clothes chill you
  • Wear a warm hat and dry socks; your camp jacket can go over you or under your feet
  • Eat a snack before bed and keep one handy: your body makes heat from food
  • Pee before you sleep: holding it keeps you colder and awake
  • Pitch away from the very bottom of a valley or a lake shore, where cold air settles
  • Take the bag out of its stuff sack as soon as the tent is up, so it can fluff up

Too cold at 2 a.m.?

Put on every layer you have, add the camp jacket over the bag, eat something, and do a few sit-ups inside the bag. Next time, choose a warmer bag or add a foam pad under the one you have.

First Backpacking Trip8
Gear

Clothing for the trail and for camp

You need two small sets: what you hike in, and a dry, warm set you only wear in camp. Avoid cotton: it stays wet and cold once it gets sweaty or rained on.

On the trail

  • Moisture-wicking shirt (synthetic or wool)
  • Hiking pants or shorts
  • Hiking socks, plus one spare pair
  • Broken-in shoes or boots
  • Light fleece or warm layer for breaks
  • Rain jacket (and rain pants if the forecast is wet)
  • Sun hat and sunglasses

In camp

  • Dry base layer top and bottom for sleeping
  • Insulated jacket (down or synthetic)
  • Warm hat
  • Dry socks for sleeping
  • Light gloves for cold evenings
  • Optional: camp shoes or sandals

Keep the camp set dry

Pack sleeping clothes in a waterproof bag or the pack liner and never hike in them. Even after a wet day, you will have something dry to change into.

Rain on the way in?

Hike in your rain jacket and quick-drying layers, and keep the camp set sealed until the tent is up. Wet socks can be dried a little inside your sleeping bag overnight, in a zip bag if they are very wet.

Check before you pack

Look at the forecast low for the campsite, not the nearest town. As a rough guide, the air often gets cooler as you climb, about 3 to 5°F for every 1,000 ft (about 5 to 9°C per 1,000 m), and it can be colder still on a clear night.

First Backpacking Trip9
Water

Water: carry, find, treat

On a day hike you carry all your water. On a backpacking trip you usually carry enough to reach the next source, then refill. That makes knowing your water sources part of the plan.

How much to carry

A common starting point is about half a liter (about 16 oz) per hour of hiking in mild weather, and more in heat, at altitude or on steep climbs. In camp you also need water for cooking, drinking and breakfast. Many people carry 2 to 3 liters between sources; carry more if sources are far apart or uncertain.

Treat every source

Clear, cold water can still carry germs that make you sick. Treat all water from lakes, streams and springs, and follow the instructions that come with your filter or tablets.

1

Filter

Squeeze, pump or gravity filters. Fast, no wait time. Keep it from freezing.

Check the filter's ratings and replace or clean it as the maker says.
2

Chemical

Tablets or drops, often chlorine dioxide. Light and simple, but you must wait.

Wait the full time on the label; cold or cloudy water can need longer.
3

Boil

Bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 ft (2,000 m).

Uses more fuel. A good backup if your filter breaks.
  • Collect from flowing or clear water; let silty water settle first
  • Keep treated and untreated water apart: mark your dirty bottle
  • Carry a backup: a few tablets weigh almost nothing
First Backpacking Trip10
Food

Food: simple is best

You will burn a lot of energy carrying a pack. First-trip food should be easy, light, filling and something you already like to eat.

No-cook

Tortillas with nut butter, hard cheese and salami, tuna or chicken packets, crackers, cold-soaked oats or couscous (soaked in a jar for an hour or two).

Boil-only

Instant oatmeal, ramen, instant mashed potatoes, couscous, freeze-dried meals. Just add boiling water and wait.

How much to pack

Many backpackers plan about 1.5 to 2 lb (0.7 to 0.9 kg) of food per person per day, choosing calorie-dense foods. Bring one extra snack meal in case you are out longer than planned.

Snack rhythm

Eat a little every hour or so instead of a few big meals: a handful of trail mix, a bar, dried fruit. Steady snacks keep your energy even and help you stay warm.

Test it at home

Cook your trail dinner at home once with your stove and pot. You will learn how much water and time it takes, and whether you like it.

  • Repack food into zip bags; take out extra boxes and wrappers
  • Pack each day's food together, labeled by meal
  • Bring a small trash bag: everything you carry in, you carry out
  • Pack a hot drink for the morning: it makes a cold camp feel easy
First Backpacking Trip11
Food

Storing food and smelly things

Bears, raccoons, mice and other animals can smell your food from far away. Protecting it keeps you safe, keeps your breakfast, and keeps wildlife wild. Animals that get human food often become a danger and may have to be removed.

1

Bear canister

A hard, locking container. Required in some parks and forests. Rent one at many parks or ranger stations.

Place it on flat ground away from cliffs and water, well away from your tent.
2

Food locker

Metal boxes at some campsites. The easiest option when they exist.

Close and latch it every time, even for a minute.
3

Hanging

A bag hung from a tree branch, away from the trunk. Allowed only in some areas and hard to do well.

Practice at home. Follow the height and distances your area gives.

What goes in

All food, trash and anything with a scent: toothpaste, sunscreen, lip balm, wipes, insect repellent, cooking pot and the dishes you used.

Follow the local rule. Each park or forest says which method is required. Look it up on the official website before you go and do exactly what it says. Some areas require a canister and do not allow hanging.
  • Never keep food or smelly items in your tent
  • Cook and eat away from where you sleep; in bear country follow the distance the area advises
  • Store food at night and whenever you leave camp
  • Count every food item back into storage before bed: one forgotten snack bar is enough
First Backpacking Trip12
Camp

Setting up camp

Aim to reach camp with at least two hours of daylight left. Pick the site, then pitch the tent before you cook.

Not to scale. Keep the kitchen and food storage away from the tent; in bear country use the distance your area advises.

Choosing the spot

  • Use an existing site or a durable surface: bare ground, gravel, rock
  • Camp at least 200 ft (about 60 m) from lakes and streams
  • Look up: no dead branches or leaning trees above
  • Avoid dips where rain will pool
  • Follow any posted rules for designated sites

Pitching the tent

  • Clear away pinecones and sharp sticks; do not dig or trench
  • Door facing away from the wind
  • Stake the corners, then attach the fly and pull it tight
  • Guy out the lines if wind or rain is expected
  • Put sleeping bag, pad and night clothes inside

Your first hour in camp

Pitch the tent, lay out the sleep system, change into dry camp clothes, fetch and treat water, then cook. Store the food before it gets dark, and put the headlamp where you can find it.

Leave No Trace

Leave No Trace for overnights

An overnight leaves more marks than a day hike: a tent site, a kitchen, a toilet. These habits, based on the Leave No Trace Seven Principles, keep the place as good as you found it.

Human waste

Use a privy where there is one. Otherwise dig a cathole 6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm) deep, at least 200 ft (about 60 m) from water, camp and trails, then cover it. Pack out toilet paper in a zip bag, or follow the local rule.

Dishes

Scrape the pot clean and eat or pack out the leftovers. Wash with a little hot water, at least 200 ft (60 m) from water, without soap or with a tiny bit of biodegradable soap. Strain bits out and pack them out, then scatter the water.

Trash

Pack it in, pack it out. All of it: food scraps, wrappers, fruit peels and tiny bits of plastic. Check the site before you leave.

Campfires

Many areas ban fires, especially in dry seasons. Check fire restrictions before every trip. A stove is better for cooking. If fires are allowed, use an existing fire ring, keep it small, and drown and stir it until it is cold to the touch.

Also

  • Stay on the trail, even when it is muddy
  • Watch wildlife from a distance and never feed it
  • Keep noise down: sound carries far at night
  • Leave rocks, plants and other natural objects as you found them

Learn more at lnt.org.

First Backpacking Trip14
Camp

Camp kitchen and stove safety

A small canister stove is the simplest stove for a first trip: screw it onto a gas canister, turn the valve, light it. Here is how to use it safely.

Never cook inside your tent

Not inside, and not in the vestibule. A stove can set the tent on fire in seconds, and it makes carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell that can kill in a closed space.

Set up

  • Practice at home first, outdoors
  • Flat, stable ground, clear of grass and leaves
  • Out of the wind, but in the open air
  • Check the canister threads and seal before screwing it on
  • Bring a lighter and a backup (matches in a zip bag)

While cooking

  • Stay with the stove the whole time it is lit
  • Keep the pot low and centered; hold the handle when stirring
  • Keep sleeves, hair and fuel away from the flame
  • Turn it off at the valve, let it cool before packing
  • Follow the stove maker's instructions

Fuel

For one or two nights, one small canister is usually plenty for one or two people boiling water for meals and drinks. Shake it to judge how full it is, and bring a new one if you are unsure. Cold weather lowers canister stove output, so keep the canister warm in your bag at night when it is cold.

Hot-water burns

Most camp kitchen injuries are spills. Sit beside the stove, not over it, and pour away from your body.

Weather, dark and when to go home

Experienced backpackers turn back all the time. Deciding early and calmly is a skill, not a failure.

Start early
MorningLeave time for slow miles, breaks and a calm camp setup.
Arrive with light
2 hoursAim for at least two hours of daylight when you reach camp.
Turnaround time
Set itIf you are not at camp by then, go back or use the backup site.

Thunderstorms

Afternoon storms are common in many mountains in summer. Plan to be off peaks, ridges and open areas by early afternoon. When you hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike: move down from high ground, away from lone tall trees, and avoid open water. Wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder before moving back up.

Darkness

Carry a headlamp and spare batteries, even if you plan to be in camp by dinner. Keep it in the top of the pack or a hip-belt pocket so you can find it in the dark.

Go home early if

The forecast turns to storms, high wind or snow; someone is hurt, sick, very cold or very tired; you cannot find safe water; or the trail is much harder than expected. Your bail-out route exists for exactly this.

Forecasts: weather.gov (US) and weather.gc.ca (Canada). Check the forecast for the trail area, not just the nearest town.

First Backpacking Trip16
Safety

Leave a trip plan, and what to do if things go wrong

Before every trip, give someone at home your plan: where you are going, who is with you, and when you will be back. If you do not return, they know when to call for help and where to send it.

A good trip plan has

  • Trailhead, route and campsite
  • Names and phone numbers of everyone in the group
  • Car make, color and license plate
  • When you will be back, and when to call for help
  • The phone number of the park, forest or local sheriff
  • Colors of your tent, pack and jacket

Tell them

Phone signal is patchy or missing on many trails. A missed text does not mean trouble, so agree on a clear "call for help by" time, a few hours after you plan to be back.

Message them as soon as you are back at the car. Do not change your route without telling them.

If you get lost

Stop. Stay calm, drink and eat something. Think about where you last knew your position. Observe: look at your map, the trail and landmarks. Plan: if you cannot work out the way back with confidence, stay where you are. People who stay put are found faster.

In an emergency call 911 (US and Canada) if you have signal. A text can sometimes get through when a call will not. If you carry a satellite messenger or your phone has satellite SOS, learn how to use it before the trip.

Know your map before you go

Study the route at home: where the water is, where the campsite is, and where the bail-out trail leaves. Download the map for offline use and carry a paper copy too. Page 25 is a fill-in trip plan you can tear out and leave at home.

First Backpacking Trip20
Packing

How to pack your pack

A well-packed bag rides close and steady. The secret: heavy things close to your back, and what you need during the day on top or in pockets.

Bottom

Bulky, light things you need only in camp: sleeping bag, sleeping clothes.

Middle, close to your back

The heaviest items: food bag or bear canister, cook kit, water reservoir. This keeps the weight centered over your hips.

Middle, away from your back

Medium items: tent body, camp jacket, fill around the heavy things.

Top and pockets

What you need on the trail: rain jacket, warm layer, first aid, water treatment, toilet kit, snacks, map, headlamp, sun gear.

Then adjust it

  • Fill gaps so nothing shifts; nothing heavy dangling outside
  • Hip belt on the hip bones first, then shoulder straps, then load lifters and sternum strap
  • Walk around the house: it should feel steady, not pulling you backward

On the trail

Loosen the shoulder straps a little on long climbs and tighten them on descents. If your shoulders ache, the hip belt is probably too loose: tighten it first.

Official sources

This guide gives you the basics. These sources have the rules, permits and conditions for the place you are going. Rules change, so check them before every trip.

National Park Service

nps.gov

Each park's "Plan Your Visit" pages list backcountry permits, food storage rules and current conditions.

USDA Forest Service

fs.usda.gov

National forests and grasslands: trails, dispersed camping rules, fire restrictions. Call the local ranger district.

Permits and reservations

recreation.gov

Backcountry permits and campsites for many US parks, forests and other public lands.

Leave No Trace

lnt.org

The Seven Principles and practical how-tos for camping, waste and campfires.

State and provincial parks

Your state or province's park site

Many have hike-in campsites that are perfect for a first trip, often bookable online.

Parks Canada

parks.canada.ca

Backcountry camping, reservations, trail reports and safety for Canada's national parks.

Weather

weather.gov and weather.gc.ca

Forecasts and warnings. Use the point forecast for the trail, not the town.

Safe drinking water

cdc.gov

CDC guidance on treating water from lakes and streams.

Words

Backpacking glossary

Base weight. Everything in your pack except food, water and fuel.

Bail-out route. A shorter way back to the road if you need to end the trip early.

Bear canister. A hard, locking food container animals cannot open.

Blaze. A paint mark or sign on trees or rocks that shows the trail.

Cathole. A small hole 6 to 8 in (15 to 20 cm) deep for human waste.

Cold soak. Soaking food in cold water instead of cooking it.

Comfort rating. The temperature at which a typical cold sleeper stays comfortable in a bag.

Dispersed camping. Camping outside designated campgrounds, where allowed.

Durable surface. Ground that is not harmed by walking or camping: rock, gravel, dry grass, bare soil.

Fly. The waterproof outer layer of a tent.

Footprint. A groundsheet under the tent that protects the floor.

Freestanding tent. A tent that stands up on its poles alone, before staking.

Guy line. A cord from the tent to a stake that keeps it steady in wind.

Hip belt. The padded belt that moves the pack's weight onto your hips.

Load lifters. Small straps from the top of the shoulder straps to the pack that pull it close.

Out-and-back. A route that returns the same way it went.

Privy. A simple backcountry toilet.

R-value. How well a sleeping pad blocks cold from the ground. Higher is warmer.

Ten Essentials. The safety items every hiker should carry, as listed by the National Park Service.

Trailhead. Where the trail starts, usually with parking.

Turnaround time. The time you decide in advance to turn back, wherever you are.

Vestibule. The covered space between the tent door and the fly.

Wag bag. A sealed bag for packing out human waste, required in some areas.

Zero day. A day of rest with no hiking on a longer trip.
